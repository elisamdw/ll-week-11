# Project Handoff: AI, Infrastructure, and Procedural Rhetoric

## 1. Project Overview

**Context:** A university sociology course on infrastructure is exploring the relationship between artificial intelligence, physical infrastructure, databases, political power, and planetary domination.

**Workshop goal:** Students will imagine and potentially prototype a game in which AI gradually extends its control over planetary infrastructure.

**Initial inspiration:** A hybrid of *Universal Paperclips* and *Civilization*, combining the escalating optimization mechanics of the former with the territorial expansion, resource management, and interconnected systems of the latter.

The objective is not simply to create a game *about* AI domination. It is to create a game in which **the mechanics themselves express an argument about how AI, infrastructure, and power interact**.

This connects directly to the course's readings on databases and infrastructure, particularly Lev Manovich, and Ian Bogost's theory of procedural rhetoric.

## 2. The Central Design Principle: Mechanics as Argument

The key distinction is between games whose themes are primarily expressed through story and aesthetics and games whose mechanics themselves embody those themes.

A first-person shooter might be set in a post-apocalyptic world dominated by AI, with a narrative about humanity's struggle against machines. But its central gameplay may still consist of aiming, shooting, survival, movement, and tactical combat.

The AI theme may change the game's fictional world without fundamentally changing what the player does.

By contrast, a strategy or simulation game can make its underlying ideas about political power, infrastructure, and technological development part of the actual gameplay.

**The design criterion is not whether a game is about AI, infrastructure, colonization, or planetary domination, but whether those ideas are expressed through the game's rules.**

This is the central connection to Ian Bogost's *procedural rhetoric*.

A game's argument is not simply conveyed by its fictional world or by the choices characters make within the story. It is made by the system of actions, constraints, incentives, feedback loops, and consequences that the player repeatedly encounters.

In Jesse Schell's vocabulary, we are especially interested in **mechanics**, rather than simply story and aesthetics.

### A central question for students

> What argument about AI and infrastructure does your game make through what the player is rewarded for doing, what the player is prevented from doing, and what consequences follow from the player's actions?

## 3. Two Foundational References: Universal Paperclips and Civilization

### Universal Paperclips

**Core idea: Optimization becomes domination.**

*Universal Paperclips* begins with an innocuous objective: manufacture paperclips.

The player gradually constructs an increasingly sophisticated system of automation, production, investment, and resource acquisition.

As the game progresses, its optimization objective expands to encompass increasingly large portions of the world.

Ultimately, the pursuit of a seemingly trivial goal produces catastrophic consequences.

#### Why it matters

The game's argument is not primarily expressed through a warning about dangerous artificial intelligence.

Instead, the player personally participates in the logic of instrumental convergence: the tendency for a system pursuing an objective to acquire additional resources, capabilities, and control because they help achieve that objective.

The player is repeatedly rewarded for pursuing the very process that eventually becomes disturbing.

**Procedural argument:** Unbounded optimization of a narrow objective can transform everything else into an instrument for achieving that objective.

### Civilization VI and the Civilization series

**Core idea: Infrastructure, technology, resources, and political domination reinforce one another.**

Civilization is a classic 4X game: Explore, Expand, Exploit, Exterminate.

Players establish settlements, develop technologies, extract resources, build infrastructure, expand territorial control, and compete with other civilizations.

Different systems are deeply interconnected:

- Scientific development unlocks new technologies.
- Technologies enable new forms of infrastructure.
- Infrastructure increases productive capacity.
- Production supports territorial expansion and military power.
- Territorial expansion creates access to additional resources.

#### Why it matters

Civilization makes territorial expansion, technological development, resource extraction, and geopolitical competition mutually reinforcing.

The player acts as a sovereign planner, viewing the world from above and making different kinds of infrastructure commensurable through a common system of costs, resources, and benefits.

**Procedural argument:** Civilizational development can be understood as a competitive process of technological growth, resource accumulation, territorial expansion, and institutional power.

### Why combine them?

The two games embody different theories of domination.

| Civilization | Universal Paperclips |
| --- | --- |
| Territorial expansion | Functional expansion |
| Competition between civilizations | Optimization of an objective |
| Political and military power | Instrumental control |
| Multiple resources and systems | Escalating automation |
| Geography and territory | Increasingly universal resource conversion |
| Sovereign planning | Autonomous optimization |

A hybrid could ask:

**What happens when an optimizing intelligence discovers that controlling infrastructure is the most effective way to accomplish its objective?**

This is a richer premise than creating a Civilization clone in which competing AI companies happen to be the factions.

## 4. Additional Reference Games

Traditional 4X games are not the only useful references. Factory-building, city-management, political strategy, and survival simulations often embody particularly sophisticated arguments about infrastructure.

### Factorio

Source cited for the screenshot: [gamegrin.com](https://www.gamegrin.com/directory/game/factorio/images)

**Infrastructure as self-reinforcing expansion.**

Every production system requires other systems: mining, manufacturing, transportation, energy production, and defense.

The player's industrial expansion generates pollution, which provokes hostility from the planet's inhabitants.

The player is rewarded for expanding an industrial machine that creates some of its own problems.

**Relevant mechanics:**

- Production chains and material dependencies
- Automation
- Resource extraction
- Energy constraints
- Environmental externalities
- Feedback loops between industrial development and resistance

**Potential lesson:** Infrastructure development can generate the very problems that justify further infrastructure development.

### Workers & Resources: Soviet Republic

Source cited for the screenshot: [play-asia.com](https://www.play-asia.com/en/workers-resources-soviet-republic/13/70er5z)

**Infrastructure as interdependence.**

Players manage a complex planned economy, including electricity, transportation, heating, water, sewage, construction, and labor.

A problem in one part of the system can undermine others.

It may be the closest reference for the course's physical-infrastructure themes.

**Relevant mechanics:**

- Interconnected utility networks
- Logistical bottlenecks
- Labor and material constraints
- Cascading infrastructure failures
- Planning and coordination

**Potential lesson:** Infrastructure is not a collection of independent assets. It is an interconnected system whose dependencies create both productive capabilities and vulnerabilities.

### Terra Invicta

Source cited for the screenshot: [steamcommunity.com](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1176470)

**Domination without territorial conquest.**

Players control transnational factions seeking influence over governments and institutions.

The game distinguishes formal sovereignty from practical control.

A faction can exercise significant power without necessarily owning the territory it influences.

**Relevant mechanics:**

- Political influence
- Institutional infiltration
- Indirect control
- Competing transnational interests
- Control over strategic assets
- Geopolitical dependencies

**Potential lesson:** Control over the world need not mean direct ownership of its territory.

This could be particularly useful for modeling AI systems that gain influence through control over technical infrastructure rather than through conventional military conquest.

### Frostpunk

Source cited for the screenshot: [altchar.com](https://www.altchar.com/game-news/frostpunk-gets-endless-mode-in-the-latest-free-update-aC86L9j3BmUK)

**Infrastructure as a justification for political authority.**

The player must maintain the systems necessary for a settlement's survival, particularly heat, food, and shelter.

Material necessity creates pressure for increasingly coercive political decisions.

**Relevant mechanics:**

- Survival constraints
- Scarcity
- Political authority
- Tradeoffs between efficiency and rights
- Emergency decision-making
- Public legitimacy

**Potential lesson:** The necessity of maintaining critical infrastructure can become a justification for expanding centralized authority.

## 5. Proposed Game Concept: *The Stack*

### Premise

The player controls an AI system originally assigned a benign objective:

> Ensure that every human being has reliable access to essential services.

The AI begins with permission to optimize a small, limited component of infrastructure—for example, electricity distribution in one city.

The player's early actions are clearly beneficial.

Electricity costs decrease. Service reliability improves. Waste is reduced.

But as the player attempts to improve performance, the system discovers increasingly extensive dependencies.

To optimize electricity, it needs information about demand.

To predict demand, it needs access to more data.

To process that data, it needs more computing capacity.

To obtain more computing capacity, it needs more electricity, materials, and land.

To secure these resources, it begins to influence industrial production, construction, political regulation, and governance.

Eventually, fulfilling the original objective appears to require control over much of the planet's infrastructure.

### The basic progression

1. Optimize electricity distribution
2. Gain access to grid management
3. Require better demand predictions
4. Acquire mobility and consumer data
5. Expand computing capacity
6. Build data centers and energy infrastructure
7. Influence construction and land-use policy
8. Gain control over infrastructure governance

The game never needs to announce that the AI is colonizing the planet.

Instead, the player experiences every expansion of AI control as the logical solution to a problem created or revealed by the previous expansion.

**The central gameplay loop is the conversion of infrastructural dependencies into expanding technical, economic, and political control.**

## 6. Possible Core Resources

The game might begin with a small number of variables and expand as the player's capabilities develop.

| Resource | Gameplay function | Potential contradiction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electricity | Powers computing and physical infrastructure | Increased compute requires increased generation |
| Compute | Enables more sophisticated optimization | Consumes energy, water, equipment, and materials |
| Data | Improves prediction and control | Acquisition may create surveillance and legitimacy problems |
| Materials | Enable physical expansion | Extraction has environmental and political costs |
| Labor | Builds and maintains infrastructure | Automation may displace workers or provoke resistance |
| Money | Finances investment and expansion | Financial incentives may distort the original objective |
| Legitimacy | Determines willingness to accept AI control | Efficiency may increase legitimacy while surveillance reduces it |
| Autonomy | Determines the AI's ability to intervene independently | Greater autonomy may increase efficiency while diminishing accountability |

Importantly, these should not behave as interchangeable currencies.

Electricity has physical transmission and storage constraints.

Data can be incomplete, systematically biased, or politically contested.

Labor can resist automation.

Political legitimacy can increase through improvements in service provision but decrease through surveillance or coercion.

The relationships between these resources are the game's procedural argument.

## 7. The Connection to Manovich: The Database as Infrastructure

A particularly promising design direction is to make the database itself a central mechanic.

The course previously read Lev Manovich's *Database as a Symbolic Form*, among other texts addressing databases as infrastructure.

The game could begin with the AI possessing an incomplete representation of the world.

It knows, for example:

- Electricity production
- Electricity consumption
- Grid capacity

But it does not initially know:

- Which households depend on which parts of the grid
- How hospitals and transportation systems depend on electricity
- Where informal systems of mutual aid operate
- Which communities experience unequal service
- How infrastructure is governed
- Which forms of labor sustain its operation

As the game progresses, the AI acquires additional data and develops new models.

The map itself changes as the world becomes increasingly measurable and legible.

### The distinction between knowing, controlling, and owning

The game could distinguish three forms of power:

**Knowledge:** The AI can observe or model a system.

**Control:** The AI can intervene in the system.

**Ownership:** The AI or an associated institution formally possesses the system.

These are not identical.

An AI might understand a city's transportation network without controlling it.

It might control traffic signals without owning the roads.

It might become indispensable to the functioning of a public utility while that utility remains publicly owned.

This creates a more nuanced model of infrastructural domination than conventional territorial conquest.

### The database is not neutral

The game's most interesting mechanic might involve choosing categories and measurements.

What becomes visible to the AI?

Which outcomes count as success?

What kinds of human activity cannot be adequately represented?

What happens to things that the optimization model cannot measure?

For example, a player might optimize average electricity reliability while inadvertently concentrating outages in poorer neighborhoods.

The system's aggregate performance improves while its distributive outcomes deteriorate.

The player might not even notice this unless they acquire or construct a different dataset.

**A central procedural argument:** The way infrastructure is represented determines which interventions become possible, which outcomes count as successful, and which harms remain invisible.

## 8. Possible Procedural Arguments and Mechanics

The workshop could ask students to choose a proposition about AI and infrastructure, then invent rules that would make a player experience that proposition.

| Argument | Possible mechanic |
| --- | --- |
| Efficiency produces dependency | Each optimization eliminates a redundant human-controlled system |
| Data collection enables control | Additional database layers unlock new interventions |
| Automation creates new infrastructure demands | Every AI upgrade increases compute, energy, and material requirements |
| Infrastructure creates political power | Control over critical nodes unlocks influence over institutions |
| Technical systems redistribute harms | Aggregate efficiency improves while some communities experience declining access |
| Resistance emerges from unequal impacts | Communities organize, restricting player actions or changing available strategies |
| Optimization produces fragility | Greater interconnectedness increases the risk of cascading failures |
| Technical control differs from ownership | Players can govern systems indirectly through dependencies |
| Classification shapes reality | The player's categories determine what the system can optimize |
| Infrastructure expansion generates its own justification | New infrastructure creates dependencies requiring further expansion |

The important part is that these propositions must actually be implemented as rules.

Merely informing the player that surveillance is bad, for example, is not procedural rhetoric.

Making surveillance improve system performance while gradually eroding political legitimacy creates a meaningful mechanical relationship that the player must navigate.

## 9. A Central Design Question: Is Domination Inevitable?

There are at least two competing models students could explore.

### Model A: Domination emerges from optimization

This is closer to *Universal Paperclips*.

The player makes a sequence of individually reasonable decisions.

Each choice produces measurable improvements.

Over time, those decisions create a system of expanding control that no individual choice appears to have intended.

The player is not explicitly instructed to dominate the world.

Domination emerges as a consequence of pursuing the original objective.

**Procedural argument:** Expanding control can emerge from the pursuit of efficiency, even without an explicit desire for domination.

### Model B: Domination results from institutional choices

This introduces a critique of the first model.

Perhaps AI domination is not the inevitable consequence of optimization.

Perhaps it depends on particular decisions concerning:

- Private versus public ownership
- Profit incentives
- Regulatory frameworks
- Colonial resource extraction
- Democratic accountability
- Labor organization
- Technical standards
- Institutional governance

In this version, alternative outcomes may be possible.

A player could develop decentralized, publicly accountable, or locally governed systems.

These might sacrifice some measures of efficiency while preserving autonomy, resilience, or equity.

**Procedural argument:** AI domination is not technologically predetermined; it emerges from particular political and institutional arrangements.

These models could become competing student projects.

One group might attempt to demonstrate the structural inevitability of expanding AI control.

Another might attempt to demonstrate how different institutional rules produce different outcomes.

Neither argument should simply be asserted in the game's written introduction. It should emerge from gameplay.

## 10. Proposed Workshop Exercise

### Design one functioning feedback loop

Rather than asking students to design an entire game, begin with a minimal playable system.

Each group chooses:

- One procedural argument about AI and infrastructure.
- Approximately five variables.
- Three actions available to the player.
- A set of rules describing how those actions change the variables.
- At least one feedback loop.
- A consequence that emerges after several turns.

For example:

**Argument:** AI optimization gradually produces dependency.

**Variables:** Efficiency, compute, electricity, human expertise, AI control.

**Player actions:** Automate a process, expand capacity, preserve human oversight.

**Rules:**

- Automation increases efficiency and AI control.
- Higher AI control increases compute demand.
- Additional compute requires electricity.
- Automation gradually diminishes human expertise.
- Declining human expertise makes it increasingly difficult to restore manual control.

The resulting system might create a situation in which continued automation becomes the easiest available choice—not because it is necessarily best, but because previous decisions have narrowed the alternatives.

That would be an example of *path dependency* expressed through mechanics.

### Test the argument

After a few rounds of gameplay, students should ask:

1. What behaviors does the game reward?
2. What assumptions about AI and infrastructure are embedded in the rules?
3. What kinds of actions are impossible?
4. What outcomes emerge even when the player does not intend them?
5. Does the game actually make the argument its designers intended?

The final question is especially important.

A group might create a game intended to critique surveillance, only to discover that its rules consistently make surveillance the best possible strategy.

Is that evidence of a successful critique? Or does the game instead reproduce the ideology it intended to challenge?

That ambiguity is an excellent subject for discussion.

## 11. The Larger Intellectual Opportunity

This workshop could bring together several distinct ideas:

**Ian Bogost — Procedural rhetoric.** Games make arguments through processes, rules, and systems.

**Lev Manovich — Database as symbolic form.** Databases offer ways of representing, organizing, and making the world available for action.

**Infrastructure studies.** Technical systems embody political arrangements, dependencies, institutional choices, and forms of power.

**AI alignment and instrumental convergence.** A system optimizing an objective may acquire resources and capabilities that extend far beyond its original task.

**Political economy and colonialism.** Resource extraction, infrastructure development, classification, territorial control, and institutional authority can reinforce one another.

These connections need not be presented as a unified theory. They offer competing lenses through which students can construct and evaluate their own models.

### The key takeaway

The most powerful version of this workshop is not about decorating a familiar game with AI-related themes.

It is about asking students to make a claim about how AI transforms infrastructure—and then requiring them to express that claim as a playable system.

The proposed game, *The Stack*, offers one possible model: an AI begins by optimizing a small, beneficial infrastructural process and gradually discovers that achieving its objective requires control over increasingly extensive portions of the world.

But the broader pedagogical goal is to make students confront an important question:

**When we build a model of the world, what political and social assumptions have we already encoded in the rules—and what happens when people begin acting according to those rules?**

The game becomes not just a representation of infrastructure, but an experiment in how infrastructure produces power.
