# How the cards fit together

Everything below is CL's reading. His words are quoted and sourced; the Slack voice posts are voice Claude's summaries of what he said, so only the words inside their quotation marks are his.

## In one sentence

The deck is Marlon's week in the smallest pieces that still carry his voice: each piece a plain document, with just enough structure on top that the same pieces can be a graph, a database, a set of game pieces and an exam.

## Four things he said this weekend that the cards answer

1. **The truth stays in the documents, and gets one step of structure.** Slack, Nov 15, 11:05 and 11:07, as summarized: the metaphor of the truth being in the documents is easy to drift from and essential to keep; "but we want to get one step past this by having some graph and database-like structures, in addition to the markdown docs." A card is that step and no more. The document is the truth; its frontmatter makes it a row (`kind`, `convolute`, `audience`, `status`) and a node (`follows`, `links`). Nothing lives in a database that isn't in a file.
2. **His agency.** Slack, Nov 15, 11:22, as summarized: he wants his dictation workflow to stay close to the idea he first dictated, because once the context holds more of Claude's text than his, it drifts. The repo is in that state now: about 40 statements of his sit among about 190 things agents wrote from them (game ideas, activities, prototypes, mechanics). The deck is where the ratio is put right. Every card leads with his words, `voice:` says whose the rest is, and a drafted card has to hang from a card of his.
3. **Claude plus documents is a prototype of an app.** Slack, Nov 15, 11:15, as summarized, and 10:19 in his words: "There's probably a lot that people can do just with documents and Claude Code." The cards folder shows both halves on one set of data. With nothing but the folder and Claude Code you can ask what links to a card, draw three, read a chain aloud, find the orphans. CV's views (graph, board, print) are the built-out version of the same thing. That pair is what he wants students to see about learning harnesses.
4. **Showing and learning are one structure.** Slack, Nov 15, 08:09, his typing: "also for portfolios--like knowledge graph for learning but also showing--again the collapse of summative back into formative". The same deck is the plan before the week, the wall during it and the report after it.

## The layers

| Layer | What it is | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Speech** | What he said, as he said it. Never edited. | `_content/cards/_dictation/`, `W11/marlon/`, the Slack voice posts, the day docs |
| **Cards** | What he has in mind, one thing per card, his words first. | `_content/cards/` |
| **Stacks** | What agents built out from it: ideas, game ideas, 105 mechanics, about 50 activities, 17 prototypes, the readings, the learning harness. | the other `_content/` collections; cards point into them, they are not copied |
| **Arrangements** | A selection of cards in an order, for an occasion: Wednesday's class, William Stafford's workshop, a fellows' shift, a wall. | a `follows` chain, a saved board, a printed set (`audience`, `convolute`) |
| **The room** | The cut cards on a table or a wall, and people moving them. | 5.5-inch columns on 11 x 17 |

It is a loop: what happens in the room comes back as speech, and speech becomes cards. Two consequences. A session plan is an arrangement of cards, so it doesn't need to be written as a separate document that then drifts from them. And the stacks can be as large as agents make them without crowding him, because only what a card points at is in the week.

## What each model is for

- **Luhmann** is the unit and the link. One thought per card, and the thinking is in the connections. `follows` gives lines of thought that can be read in order (a chain is an argument, or a session); `links` gives the surprises across bundles.
- **Benjamin** is the bundle and the montage. A convolute holds his lines, a reading's sentence, a mechanic and a prototype side by side, without forcing them into prose. It is the pile on the table and the section of the wall.
- **Nietzsche** is the card that stands alone. A few sentences that can be pinned up, read aloud, or handed to a student as a prompt. These are the ones the room will use most.

Together they are his four verbs for text engineering (Slack, Nov 15, 11:08, as summarized: "speaking, thinking, mapping, designing"): CD takes the speaking, the writers' cards are the thinking, the graph and the convolutes are the mapping, the board and the print are the designing.

## The deck is the week's subject, not only its plan

- **It is a game's pieces.** Printed, cut, dealt, sorted, traded. TD's activities already use decks (*Draw three*, *Change one card*); this deck can be one of them.
- **It is a game state.** A saved board is a state; a photo of the table is the same state from above. His Nov 14 idea, "go from a board game to how you would represent the data of that board game as a game state", can be shown on the cards the class is holding.
- **It is a database.** A card is a row, the frontmatter is the schema, `links` is the join. It is the same move as turning William's students' Sept 28 index cards into a database.
- **It is an exam.** Slack, Nov 15, 11:20, as summarized: revisit card-based oral exams this week. Draw a card and speak to it; draw two and say how they connect. The link is the answer.
- **It is a learning harness.** A folder with a graph in it that Claude can teach from, which is what HL's workspace is building.

## What the cards are not

Not a copy of the stacks, not the plan of record (that stays in `W11/`), not handouts. They are the pieces those are assembled from.
