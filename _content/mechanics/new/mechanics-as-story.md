# Mechanics tell the story

## Definition

What the player can do (the game's verbs and their limits) carries its meaning and plot, apart from its text and images. Bogost calls arguments made this way procedural rhetoric. [2]

In one line (EG's row): "What the player can do (the verbs) carries the game's meaning and plot, apart from its text." [1]

## Description

In games:

- **[Papers, Please](../games/papers-please.md)**: The player is a border inspector checking documents against a growing list of rules, and stamping papers brings constant moral choices that make it hard to be good. [3]
- **[Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons](../games/brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons.md)**: Each brother is steered with one side of the controller, and late in the game that division of the controls becomes part of the story's ending. [4]
- **[Animal Crossing](../games/animal-crossing.md)**: Paying off the house loan earns a bigger house and a bigger loan, so the game argues about debt without saying a word (Bogost, as quoted in the glossary). [2]

**Shows it** (EG's row): Papers, Please; Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons; The Legend of Zelda (the sword as the first gift) [1]

**With AI** (EG's row): What does a game about AI let the humans do? The verbs are its argument. [1]

**In our game** (Nov 14 rulesets): yes: the factions' actions (harden, protest, sell) are the game's claims [1]

## How to

**Try it in two minutes** (EG's row): Take a story everyone knows and give it one verb; play it, then change the verb. [1]

**Build it** (EG's row): Change what the player can do at a turning point in the story, not what the screen says. [1]
