# Goal and win condition

## Definition

The condition, set before play starts, that ends the game and says who has won, lost or drawn. Because it names what players are after, it decides which moves count as good ones.

On GM's card: "The state that ends the game and says who won." [1]

## Description

In games:

- **[Chess](../games/chess.md)**: Checkmate ends the game at once, whatever material either side still has, so a player can be far ahead and still lose. [2]
- **[Monopoly](../games/monopoly.md)**: The last player who is not bankrupt wins, so the game is won by ruining the others rather than by getting rich. [3]
- **[Pandemic](../games/pandemic.md)**: The players win together by curing all four diseases, and lose together in any of three ways (too many outbreaks, too few disease cubes, too few player cards left to draw). [4]
- **[Data Center](../games/data-center.md)**: The AI wins when it holds half the region's nodes; the humans survive if the ten rounds run out first, and then check each faction's private goal. [5]

**Shows it** (GM's card): Chess (checkmate) [1]

**With AI** (HI): Who sets the AI's goal, and can anyone change it? A fixed, trivial goal is the paperclip maximizer; a goal the players must name is alignment. [1]

**In our game** (Nov 14 rulesets): yes: every version (Data Center ends on the AI's share of the map or the last round; The Committee and Normal Technology the same way) [1]

## How to

**Try it in two minutes** (GM's card): Play tic-tac-toe, then change the goal: whoever makes three in a row loses. [1]

**Build it** (GM's card): A `/stage` page that watches one number and fires an end cue when it is reached. [1]

## Why to

**Proposed, for Marlon to choose:**

1. Rewrite a familiar game's goal (tic-tac-toe where three in a row loses, as GM's card has it) to show students that the goal, not the pieces, sets what the game is about.
2. In a game about AI, ask who wrote the AI's goal and who can change it; the win condition is where the alignment question becomes a rule.
3. Use it as the first question of a close reading: what does this game count as winning, and what does that leave out?

## Home game version

### In a regular classroom

**Proposed, for Marlon to choose:**

1. Play any short game twice, once with its goal and once with an inverted goal, and compare how people played.
2. Groups write a win condition on an index card for a shared board; the class plays whichever card is drawn.

### Digitally

**Proposed, for Marlon to choose:**

1. A one-number web page (a shared counter) with an end condition the class sets before play.
2. Ask Claude to change only the win condition of a small browser game and play the two versions side by side.
