# The overhead view

## Definition

A camera mounted above a table and pointed straight down, so that paper, objects and the hands working on them become a flat, readable picture: shown live on a screen for the room, sent to a call, or recorded.

## Description

The overhead view turns a tabletop into a frame. Whatever is laid on the table (a drawing, cut-up film stills, cards, a game board, a hand building something) is seen by everyone at once and can be recorded as it changes. Marlon, Nov 17, 07:13 (voice agent's record), asks of it "how it's valuable, which contexts you might want it in, and ways of achieving it outside the Learning Lab space" [1].

Sessions where the LL used it, from the record:

- **SCRB 111, Explainer Video Workshop, Sept 16, 2025.** Groups took a section of a research paper and "translated that section into a paper prototype + overhead camera for an explainer video segment" [3][4].
- **HUM 7, planned Oct 7, 2025.** Students do "brief formal analyses using cut-up film stills under an overhead camera, presenting a short mock video essay that rehearses the same analytical moves they'll use in their written papers" [5]. (A plan; the record I read does not say how it went.)
- **A workshop on nonverbal communication and visuals** (undated plan). The welcome is given at the overhead ("use overhead to visually facilitate welcome"), and in the visuals activity a few volunteers present "at the overhead" [6].
- **The three-station video essay workshop** (GENED 1145 and others), Station 1: "Students create visual arguments using overhead cameras" [7]. This comes from the Greatest Hits report, a model's synthesis of the LL's 2021–25 HackMD notes, not a first-hand record.
- **FYSEMR 68D, looms, Oct 1, 2026.** "students build looms, hammering in nails; overhead camera" [8]. The outcome is not recorded there.
- **TDM 155AI, Nov 11, 2026.** *Draw a box*: under the overhead camera, a student draws a rectangle in thick dark marker and the app plays a clip inside it; listed as optional, "only if the overhead station passed the morning test" [9][10].
- **Week 11, Nov 14 to 16, 2026.** Marlon proposed photographing the LL's board games from overhead before play and mid-game, to show what game state is [11]. On Nov 16 "Blue and Jordan set up four overhead cameras today" (17:07), and Marlon, in a typed post at 13:23, called four overhead cameras "an LL Mechanic" for "various multi-station gamified learning activities" [2] (see [Four overhead cameras](overhead-stations.md)).
- The LLUFs have also tested surfaces for it: "New Mechanic: chalk marker on dark paper under overhead" [12].

## How to

In the studio, from the record:

1. **Mount and frame.** The camera looks straight down at the table, the paper filling the frame with "slight border for context" [13]. Marlon's task for this week is "always-on positions that are predictable and reliable, with cameras mounted on the truss in both locations", plus "tabletop or truss-top cameras across the hall" [1].
2. **Light it evenly.** For top-down shots: "Even, soft lighting (overhead softbox or diffused natural light); minimize shadows" [13].
3. **Route it to the room.** The overhead is a camera input on the studio's switcher; the nonverbal workshop's plan sends the classroom TV's output to the overhead ("start with it on the OH, which i think is camera 2") and switches to a laptop later [6].
4. **Check the picture in the app you will use.** The Nov 11 class app asks you to "pick the overhead camera (Mirror off)" [10]; its run-of-show tests "overhead camera, dark rectangle, clip selection, stability under the room's light" in the morning [9].
5. **Record or grab stills.** For documentation stills from an overhead shot, the LL's stills workflow says: "have one with hands and one without hands" [14].

What goes wrong, from the record: shadows and uneven light [13]; a mirrored image when a browser treats the camera as a selfie camera [10]; computer vision that depends on the room's light [9]; and a tilted board, which puts reading pins "on the wrong spaces" (proposed fix: printed corner markers) [15]. Blurry hands are common enough that the stills workflow plans around them [14].

## Why to

From the record:

- **Ideas before software.** The Greatest Hits report (a synthesis, see above) says paper under the overhead "Removes 'I don't know the software' barrier" and makes iteration fast: "draw, photograph, adjust = 2 minutes" [7].
- **A rehearsal of the writing.** HUM 7's plan uses the overhead's mock video essay to rehearse "the same analytical moves they'll use in their written papers" [5].
- **Translating research for an audience.** SCRB 111 used it to turn a paper into an explainer segment [3].
- **Presenting.** The welcome and the volunteers' visuals at the overhead in the nonverbal workshop [6].
- **Many tables at once.** Marlon, Nov 16, 13:23 (typed): "one that we frequently use for various multi-station gamified learning activities" [2].
- **Seeing a game's state.** Marlon's Nov 14 plan for the board games [11].

**Proposed, for Marlon to choose:** which of these the entry leads with for this week's rooms.

1. Teaching: the overhead as the shared table, where a group's paper work becomes the room's text to discuss (SCRB 111, HUM 7).
2. Presenting: the overhead as the presenter's hands, drawing or arranging while speaking (the nonverbal workshop).
3. Games: the overhead as the referee's eye on the board, photographing state for a model to read (Nov 14 and TDM 155AI tomorrow).

## Home game version

### In a regular classroom

**Proposed, for Marlon to choose:**

1. A phone on a stand or arm above the desk, joined to a video call that the room's projector shows. (We suggest this; the LL's record has no session that did it.)
2. The room's own document camera, where the room has one. (We have not checked which Harvard rooms do.)
3. No live feed: photograph the table from above at set moments and project the photos, as in the photograph-the-state plan [11].

### Digitally

- **Apple's Desk View.** A Mac with an iPhone as its camera (Continuity Camera) can show the desk in front of it from above, beside your face (Apple's page title: "Use Desk View to show your face and desk on camera"). Desk View needs a "Mac using Continuity Camera with iPhone 11 or later, excluding iPhone 16e and iPhone SE models", or one of the newer Macs with a built-in camera that supports it; on macOS Sonoma or later it is opened "From the Video menu in the menu bar, choose Desk View", and it is shared through the video app's screen sharing [16]. Continuity Camera itself needs "macOS Ventura 13 or later" and "iOS 16 or later" [17]. Not tested by the LL.
- We suggest a second device in the call (a phone pointed down, muted) as the overhead feed in any video-call app; we have not confirmed a vendor page for this.
