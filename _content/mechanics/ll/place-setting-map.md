# The place-setting map

## Definition

A single large sheet, 11×17 inches, set in front of each person or pair like a place setting at a table: a printed or hand-drawn map of the task, which is the surface they write on, lay cards and pieces onto, and take away.

## Description

Marlon, Nov 17, 07:13 to 07:14 (voice agent's record): "Just cards. 11×17 paper is the technology, and the mechanic could be sort of the map, the table... the place setting... kind of place-setting-size map, essentially", to be achieved "physically, in regular classes, and then in digital ways" [1]. The sheet gives each seat its own territory on a shared table: big enough to hold a layout of cards, a diagram, a board, small enough to be one person's.

What the record shows nearby (a per-seat 11×17 map in a named session was not found in what I read):

- **The agenda on 11×17.** A workshop on nonverbal communication and visuals (undated plan): "use an 11 x 17 to write out the agenda for the workshop, modeling that tip" [2].
- **11×17 as the LL's print size.** Marlon's card print spec, Nov 15, 2026: "5.5" wide (a column of 11x17 vertically" [3]; "He prints on 11x17 (and Letter), cuts the cards, and uses them on tables and walls" [4]; the studio's Ricoh takes 11×17 in Tray 3 [5]; The Notebook's printing skill prints "by default 11x17" [6].
- **Maps on the table for a games session.** ENGL 189VG, Oct 26, 2026, prep: "large rolls of paper / game boards / video game maps printed (a couple large?)" [7].
- **Annotating a map.** SOC-STD 98MR, spring 2025: "students annotated satellite images of Harvard Yard and Cambridge, mapping binaries like 'home/exclusion' or 'open/surveilled' onto familiar terrain"; NEC 101, spring 2025, at wall scale (see [Map on the projected wall](projected-wall-map.md)) [8].
- **In games.** The week's research on strategy games notes *The Quiet Year* (Avery Alder, 2013), where "players draw a shared community map" [9].

## How to

1. **Decide what the sheet is a map of.** A board for a game, a template (a timeline, a grid, a dialogue tree's slots), a printed image to annotate, or a blank sheet with a frame.
2. **Print one per seat** on 11×17 (the studio's Ricoh, Tray 3 [5]; The Notebook's `mk-printing` skill for Markdown [6]). Portrait or landscape to suit the map.
3. **Set the table.** Sheet, cards, markers; leave room around it for the cards that are not yet placed.
4. **Capture it.** Each sheet fits the overhead camera's frame, so the arrangement can be shown to the room or photographed (see [The overhead view](overhead-view.md)).

What goes wrong: we have no record of it run this way yet. We expect cards to overhang a sheet when the deck is large (Marlon's cards are 5.5 inches wide [3], half the sheet's short side), and green ink or paper to vanish under a keyed camera (see [Green paper](green-paper-key.md)).

## Why to

Marlon's words give the shape (a map, a table, a place setting [1]) but not yet the teaching purpose. The record has the general case for printing things for the room: "inventories of game mechanics that are printable and displayable throughout the space" (Marlon, Nov 13) [10].

**Proposed, for Marlon to choose:**

1. **Each person's own board.** In a game, the sheet is the player's mat (resources, hand, secret information), and the table between the sheets is the shared board: the Stafford game's per-player state on paper.
2. **A template that carries the method.** The sheet is a printed scaffold (a timeline, a database schema's columns, a dialogue tree), so the activity's structure is in front of every student without instructions on the screen.
3. **A take-away record.** What a student builds on the sheet goes home with them, or is photographed for the class archive, so the map is both workspace and handout.

## Home game version

### In a regular classroom

**Proposed, for Marlon to choose:**

1. The same sheet on Letter or A4 (half the area), printed on any office printer, with cards cut smaller.
2. A tabloid printer is not needed: two Letter sheets taped side by side make 11×17 (17 by 11 inches).

### Digitally

**Proposed, for Marlon to choose:**

1. A PDF of the map that students annotate on a tablet or laptop and submit.
2. A shared online board, one frame per student or pair, the digital place setting; the instructor's view shows all frames at once.
