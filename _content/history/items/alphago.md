# AlphaGo, AlphaZero, and MuZero (2015–2020)

Part of [Strand 1: Games as AI's proving ground](../1-proving-ground.md) · [Timeline](../timeline.md)

**Fan Hui, October 2015.** AlphaGo beat the three-time European champion 5–0, the first AI win against a Go professional on a full board without handicap. The paper, "Mastering the game of Go with deep neural networks and tree search", appeared in *Nature* on January 27, 2016.

**Lee Sedol, Seoul, March 9–15, 2016.** AlphaGo won 4–1 at the Four Seasons Hotel. DeepMind says its Move 37 in game 2 "had a 1 in 10,000 chance of being used"; Lee's Move 78 in game 4, the one human win, had the same odds, and Gu Li called it a "divine move". AlphaGo was made an honorary 9-dan.

**Master and Ke Jie.** Playing online as "Master" (late December 2016 to January 2017), AlphaGo went 60–0, including three wins over Ke Jie; at the Future of Go Summit in Wuzhen in May 2017 it beat Ke 3–0 and was retired. Ke: "not a single human has touched the edge of the truth of Go."

**Self-play, then no rules.** AlphaGo Zero (*Nature*, October 19, 2017) learned with no human games and beat the version that played Lee 100–0. AlphaZero (arXiv December 5, 2017; *Science* December 7, 2018) did the same for chess and shogi. MuZero (arXiv November 19, 2019; *Nature* December 23, 2020) learned a model of the rules instead of being given them.

**Lee's retirement, November 19, 2019:** "Even if I become the number one, there is an entity that cannot be defeated." In a 2024 *New York Times* interview he said "losing to AI, in a sense, meant my entire world was collapsing" (both quoted via Wikipedia).

**The footnote.** In 2022–23, FAR AI found a "cyclic" attack on superhuman Go programs; Kellin Pelrine, an amateur co-author, learned it and "repeatedly beat superhuman versions of both KataGo and Leela Zero." Superhuman is not robust.

**Why it matters.** Go was the game experts expected to hold out for another decade. Move 37 is the standard example of machine creativity, and the line from AlphaGo to MuZero traces the field's turn from human data, to self-play, to learning the rules.
