# Deep Blue v. Kasparov (1996–1997)

Part of [Strand 1: Games as AI's proving ground](../1-proving-ground.md) · [Timeline](../timeline.md)

**The lineage.** Feng-hsiung Hsu began ChipTest at Carnegie Mellon in 1985; its successor, Deep Thought, reached a USCF rating of 2551 in 1988. Hsu and Murray Campbell joined IBM Research in 1989, Deep Thought lost two games to Kasparov that year, and the machine was renamed Deep Blue.

**1996, Philadelphia (February 10–17).** Deep Blue won game 1, the first win by a computer over a reigning world champion under classical time controls. Kasparov won the match 4–2.

**1997, New York (May 3–11, Equitable Center).** Deep Blue, now searching about 200 million positions a second, won 3½–2½. After game 2 Kasparov alleged human intervention, pointing to the move 37.Be4. IBM declined a rematch and dismantled the machine; one rack is at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History and the other at the Computer History Museum.

**Why it matters.** It is the iconic "machine beats humanity" moment and the end point of Shannon's 1950 program of search plus evaluation. It is also the clearest case of a game result standing in for intelligence: the method, special-purpose search hardware, carried over to little else. John McCarthy's review in *Science* that June ("AI as Sport") argued that computer chess had become sport rather than science. It was IBM's second corporate game showcase, after Arthur Samuel's checkers and before Watson.
