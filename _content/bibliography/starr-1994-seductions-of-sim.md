# Seductions of Sim

**Citation:** Starr, Paul. "Seductions of Sim: Policy as a Simulation Game." *The American Prospect*, no. 17 (Spring 1994): 19–29.

**What it argues:** Starr, a Princeton sociologist who had worked on the Clinton health plan, sets *SimCity* beside the Congressional Budget Office's scoring models: both teach people to manage interconnected systems, and both hide their assumptions. "The critical problem raised by simulation is the black-box nature of the models" (prospect.org, seen); whoever controls a model few can inspect gains power over policy, so models should be open and no single one should rule.

**Why it's canonical:** It is the early critique of simulation's hidden assumptions that game-design courses assign beside the simulation games themselves (MIT CMS.608) and that the first political-science readings of *SimCity* cite; Bogost's simulation gap works the same ground.

**Key terms:** black-box models, simulation as policy, the politics of a model's assumptions.

**For the game (TH proposal):** (editor) No black boxes: every ruleset in the editor shows its assumptions and a `notModeled` list at the end of play, and the debrief asks who in the room could get an assumption changed (HI's `not-modeled`).
