# Playing Subaltern: Video Games and Postcolonialism

**Citation:** Mukherjee, Souvik. "Playing Subaltern: Video Games and Postcolonialism." *Games and Culture* 13, no. 5 (2018): 504–520. https://doi.org/10.1177/1555412015627258 (online first February 9, 2016). See also Mukherjee, *Videogames and Postcolonialism: Empire Plays Back* (Cham: Palgrave Macmillan, 2017), https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-54822-7.

**What it argues:** From the abstract (seen via OpenAlex; the article itself was not opened): the postcolonial "has still remained on the margins of Game Studies," yet for Indian players of *Empire: Total War* or *East India Company* the encounter with colonial history is "direct and unavoidable." Games build their space, politics, and ethics on a colonial notion that also invites its questioning, and the article reads those undertones from the position of players in the formerly colonized world. The book (not opened here) carries the argument further; its subtitle, *Empire Plays Back*, names the stance.

**Why it's canonical:** It is one of the founding articles of postcolonial game studies, assigned in Alberta's 2018 course on games and culture, answered in the 2018 special issue Mukherjee co-edited, and cited over a hundred times; his chapter "Age of Empires: Postcolonialism" is also assigned (Toronto's INF2255 Critical Game Studies, 2023).

**Key terms:** postcolonial game studies, the subaltern, empire plays back, colonial spatiality, the 4X map as colonial map.

**For the game (TH proposal):** (B, debrief) The map's nodes start as places with names and residents, not empty land; when the AI converts one, the card records who lived there, and one faction may play the colonized side whose win is keeping its own node, not taking the map.
