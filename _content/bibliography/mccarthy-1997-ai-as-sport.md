# AI as Sport

**Citation:** McCarthy, John. "AI as Sport." Review of *Kasparov versus Deep Blue: Computer Chess Comes of Age*, by Monty Newborn (New York: Springer, 1996). *Science* 276, no. 5318 (June 6, 1997): 1518–19.

**What it argues:** Published a month after Deep Blue beat Kasparov in their 1997 rematch, the review by the man who named artificial intelligence says computer chess has traded science for sport: "the fixation of most computer chess work on success in tournament play has come at scientific cost." Had geneticists bred racing fruit flies, "We would have *some* science, but mainly we would have very fast fruit flies" (Stanford copy, seen). With machines at world-champion level, chess should become AI's *Drosophila* again, a model organism for understanding how intelligence works with far less computation.

**Why it's canonical:** It is the founder's verdict on the first great machine-versus-human game, the standard source for the distinction between AI as benchmark and AI as science, and it frames every later "AI beats game" headline (AlphaGo, Cicero); it is adjacent because it is cited more in AI history than in game studies.

**Key terms:** chess as the *Drosophila* of AI, AI as sport, benchmark versus understanding, brute-force search.

**For the game (TH proposal):** (B, debrief) The AI grows by winning named benchmark games, each win adding a capability card and a point of investment, and the debrief asks McCarthy's question: did any win teach the humans how the AI thinks, or only make a faster fruit fly?
