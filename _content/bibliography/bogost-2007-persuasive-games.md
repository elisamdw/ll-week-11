# Persuasive Games

**Citation:** Bogost, Ian. *Persuasive Games: The Expressive Power of Videogames*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2007.

**What it argues:** Games make arguments through their rules. Bogost calls this **procedural rhetoric**, persuasion through "rule-based representations and interactions" (p. ix, as quoted in RS's notes; not seen here), and studies it across the book's three parts, politics, advertising, and learning. Rules define a **possibility space** that play explores, so a rule set argues by what it allows, rewards, and forbids; the book closes on **procedural literacy**, learning to read and write such arguments.

**Why it's canonical:** It is the most assigned statement of games-as-argument (MIT CMS.300's "Value Systems" session, Swarthmore FMST 86), the book that Sicart's "Against Procedurality" (2011) answers, and Stafford's own reading for the Nov 23 workshop.

**Key terms:** procedural rhetoric, procedurality, possibility space, procedural literacy, anti-advergame, simulation and ideology.

**For the game (TH proposal):** the editor's "rhetoric" field is Bogost's term made into a form: students state the argument each rule makes, play it, and in the debrief check whether the rules argued what the field claims (editor, debrief).
