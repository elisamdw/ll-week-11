# Game Design as Narrative Architecture

**Citation:** Jenkins, Henry. "Game Design as Narrative Architecture." In *First Person: New Media as Story, Performance, and Game*, edited by Noah Wardrip-Fruin and Pat Harrigan, 118–130. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2004. Online at *Electronic Book Review*, July 10, 2004. (Page range unverified.)

**What it argues:** Jenkins takes a middle path in the ludology/narratology fight: not every game tells a story, but many games tell stories through space, as theme parks and travel writing do. "Spatial stories are not badly constructed stories" (ebr, seen). He names four ways space carries narrative: **evoked** (calling up a story the player already knows), **enacted** (the player moves through story events), **embedded** (story details planted in the world to be discovered), and **emergent** (stories players build out of systems, as in *The Sims*). The designer works as an architect of narrative spaces more than as a storyteller.

**Why it's canonical:** It is in both *First Person* and *The Game Design Reader*, it is the usual reading for "game spaces" and "environmental storytelling" (MIT, Waterloo, Swarthmore, Davidson), and it drew public replies from Eskelinen and McKenzie on ebr.

**Key terms:** narrative architecture, spatial stories, environmental storytelling, evoked, enacted, embedded, and emergent narrative.

**For the game (TH proposal):** embedded narrative on the map: each node from the students' index-card database keeps a one-line history that is revealed when the AI's data center reaches it, so the spread uncovers the place it consumes (B).
