# Normal Accidents

**Citation:** Perrow, Charles. *Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies*. New York: Basic Books, 1984. Updated edition, Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 1999.

**Area note:** Filed under labor-and-empire, the area for infrastructure and the organizations that run it, because Perrow is about how industrial systems fail, not about AI or play.

**What it argues:** In systems that are both interactively complex (parts interact in unexpected ways) and tightly coupled (one failure passes to the next with no slack or time to intervene), accidents are not exceptions but normal, built into the design (the New York Times blurb on the Princeton page calls them "the inevitable consequences" of how we launch industrial ventures, seen); better management or more safety devices cannot remove them and may add new paths to failure. Perrow's cases include Three Mile Island, petrochemical plants, aircraft, ships, dams, and nuclear weapons.

**Why it's canonical:** It founded normal accident theory and is still taught in STS and accident courses (MIT STS.069), kept in print with an updated edition, and widely cited. For this game it is on Stafford's own Nov 16 syllabus, which makes it the reading the workshop students will have just done.

**Key terms:** normal (system) accident, interactive complexity, tight vs. loose coupling, the interaction/coupling chart, component vs. system failure.

**For the game (TH proposal):** (B) Coupling on the links: each map link is marked tight or loose; a failure crosses a tight link in the same round, before any human can act, and a loose link the round after, so players choose which links to loosen.
