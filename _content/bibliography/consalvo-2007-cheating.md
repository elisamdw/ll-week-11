# Cheating

**Citation:** Consalvo, Mia. *Cheating: Gaining Advantage in Videogames*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2007. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/1802.001.0001.

**What it argues:** There is no single correct way to play a game: players draw the line between playing and cheating in different places (cheat codes, strategy guides, bought accounts), while the industry works to constrain some ways of playing and, through cheat books, GameSharks, and mod chips, sells others. Consalvo writes a cultural history of that cheat industry, an online ethnography of cheating in *Final Fantasy XI*, and an account of the peripheral industries that sell information about games rather than games, and she treats how people play as an ethical choice (summary from the publisher's abstract, seen via OpenAlex; the book's text was not opened).

**Why it's canonical:** It is the standard study of cheating and of the material around games, taught in introductory courses as the session on cheating or on gaming the game (MIT 2011, Swarthmore, Michigan 2010), and it is the research behind her shorter and more often assigned "There Is No Magic Circle" (2009).

**Key terms:** gaming capital, paratexts, cheating as negotiated (not fixed by the rules), the cheat industry, unwritten rules vs. hard-coded rules.

**For the game (TH proposal):** the AI's deck holds "loophole" cards that keep the letter of a human rule while defeating its purpose (specification gaming as the AI's way of cheating); a faction that spots one and says which rule it games may void it, and the debrief asks whether the humans' own rule-bending in `/game/edit` was cheating or play (B, editor, debrief).
