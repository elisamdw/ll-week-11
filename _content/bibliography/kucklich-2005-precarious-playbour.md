# Precarious Playbour: Modders and the Digital Games Industry

**Citation:** Kücklich, Julian. "Precarious Playbour: Modders and the Digital Games Industry." *The Fibreculture Journal*, no. 5 (2005), "Precarious Labour" issue, FCJ-025.

**What it argues:** Modding extends a game's shelf life and supplies the innovation the industry will not risk (*Counter-Strike*, a *Half-Life* mod later sold on its own, is the case), yet modders are rarely paid; their work is veiled as leisure, producing "a hybrid form of “playbour”" (para. 5, seen). Borrowing Terranova's free labor, Kücklich argues modding fits neither wage work nor play, that restrictive end-user license agreements bar modders from selling what they make, and that the figure of the empowered "prosumer" hides the power structures modders work within.

**Why it's canonical:** It named playbour, the term game studies still uses for value produced by play, and it is assigned in game-studies seminars on labor and the material turn (Victoria 2022, Stark's model syllabus) and cited about five hundred times.

**Key terms:** playbour, modding, precarious labour, free labour, EULA, the modding community as a dispersed multitude.

**For the game (TH proposal):** (editor, debrief) Every ruleset a student saves in `/game/edit` and the table adopts adds one point to a public "playbour" counter; the debrief asks who owns the improved game and what the authors were paid, the same question Kücklich asks of modders.
