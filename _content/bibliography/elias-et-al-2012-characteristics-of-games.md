# Characteristics of Games

**Citation:** Elias, George Skaff, Richard Garfield, and K. Robert Gutschera. *Characteristics of Games*. Foreword by Eric Zimmerman. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2012.

**What it argues:** Three working designers (Garfield made *Magic: The Gathering*) compare games of every kind, board, card, sport, and computer, by the traits they share rather than by genre: length, number of players, luck and skill, hidden information, reward for effort. The contents (UW-Madison catalog, seen) run from basics and multiplayer politics through "games as systems" (snowball and catch-up, game arc, strategic collapse) to the metagame, spectators, and misbehavior, with appendixes on von Neumann and combinatorial game theory. Zimmerman's foreword singles out their attempt to rate the luck and skill in chess, poker, and roulette numerically and the "Catch-Up," the paradox that players must keep hope of a reversal while every move still counts (seen). The book's own text was not opened.

**Why it's canonical:** It is the designers' analytic handbook beside *Rules of Play*: Zimmerman wrote its foreword, and a Berkeley seminar on speculative world-building through board games made it the source "we will repeatedly return to" (2018). It is less often assigned in game-studies surveys than the core texts, hence important, not core.

**Key terms:** snowball and catch-up, kingmaking, player elimination, luck and skill as separate axes, heuristics, game arc, strategic collapse, downtime and busywork, reward/effort ratio, metagame, spectation, misbehavior.

**For the game (TH proposal):** (B, debrief) the AI's data centers are a snowball and the humans need a catch-up: when the AI holds a third of the map the regulator gains an action each round; the debrief names the kingmaker, the faction that could no longer win but chose who did (the utility selling it power).
