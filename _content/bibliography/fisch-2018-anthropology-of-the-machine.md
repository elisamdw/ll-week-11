# An Anthropology of the Machine

**Citation:** Fisch, Michael. *An Anthropology of the Machine: Tokyo's Commuter Train Network*. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2018. Assigned: chap. 6, "Ninety Seconds," 206–259.

**What it argues:** Tokyo's commuter network is one of the most complex technical infrastructures on Earth, and it runs beyond its nominal capacity because commuters, drivers, and control systems (JR East's ATOS, an "autonomous decentralized" traffic control system) absorb the strain together, at a physical and emotional cost (publisher page and Calimente's review, seen). Fisch's key idea is the margin of indeterminacy, the slack that lets a system absorb change. Per Calimente, the final chapter, the assigned "Ninety Seconds," takes up the April 25, 2005 Amagasaki derailment (107 dead): a driver pressing to make up a delay took a packed train into a curve too fast, under a regime that punished drivers for lateness. Wikipedia (orientation only) says the train had been 90 seconds behind schedule, which the chapter title presumably names; we did not open the chapter.

**Why it's canonical:** Not a game-studies text, and young, so adjacent; but it is widely reviewed in anthropology and Japan studies and cited about a hundred times, and it is Stafford's own pairing with Perrow on Nov 16, the week before the workshop.

**Key terms:** operation beyond capacity, the interval, the margin of indeterminacy, recovery driving, ATOS (autonomous decentralized control), human-machine collective life.

**For the game (TH proposal):** (B) Slack on the links: each link on the class's index-card map has a capacity, and running it over capacity pays more but leaves a delay token; a faction that does not clear its delays by the next round must make them up at speed, and a link that is both over capacity and behind schedule fails (Perrow's tight coupling with the human pressure put back in).
