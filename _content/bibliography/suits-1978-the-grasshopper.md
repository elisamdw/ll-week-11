# The Grasshopper

**Citation:** Suits, Bernard. *The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia*. Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1978. Third edition, with an introduction by Thomas Hurka. Peterborough, ON: Broadview Press, 2014.

**What it argues:** Against Wittgenstein's claim that "game" cannot be defined, Suits defines it through four elements: a goal that can be named apart from the game, means limited by rules, rules that forbid the more efficient means, and players who accept those rules because they make the activity possible. His short form is that playing a game is "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles" (as quoted in the SEP entry, seen; p. 41 of the 1978 edition per a review, unverified). The dialogue ends in a utopia where, with all work done by machines, game-playing is what is left to want.

**Why it's canonical:** The SEP calls it "the seminal formalist text" in the philosophy of sport, Juul tests its definition beside Huizinga's and Caillois's, and Broadview keeps it in print with a new critical introduction. Its utopia has a second life in arguments about AI and a world without work, which is where Bostrom's *Deep Utopia* meets it.

**Key terms:** prelusory goal, lusory goal, lusory means, constitutive rules, lusory attitude, unnecessary obstacles, the utopia of games.

**For the game (TH proposal):** (A, B) the AI plays without the lusory attitude: it pursues the prelusory goal by the most efficient means and is not bound by the constitutive rules the human factions accept, so the ruleset gives the AI's deck a list of human rules it may ignore, each with its `rhetoric` line.
