# Autonomous Technology

**Citation:** Winner, Langdon. *Autonomous Technology: Technics-out-of-Control as a Theme in Political Thought*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1977. Assigned: chap. 5, "Artifice and Order," 173–236.

**What it argues:** Winner takes seriously the old fear that technology has escaped human control, from Ellul's autonomous technique to the everyday sense that the next system must be adopted, and treats it as a question for political theory rather than a mood to dismiss. His claim is not that machines have wills but that large technical systems come to set their own requirements, and people adjust to them: his term "reverse adaptation" names the adjustment of human ends to fit the available means. "Artifice and Order" is the chapter where he follows that adjustment into the order of society; one reader quotes it as saying "Technical artifice as an aggregate phenomenon dwarfs human consciousness" (p. 190, as quoted in Beebe's Substack, Jan 11, 2026; seen there, unverified against the book). We did not open the book; this summary comes from the SEP entry, published reading notes on the introduction, and secondary descriptions.

**Why it's canonical:** The SEP places Winner among the founders of political philosophy of technology, the book drew reviews from figures such as Mesthene on publication, it has an entry in a German reference volume on the field, and Winner was still answering for it in a 2020 interview, the year he shared the Bernal Prize (ESTS). For this game it is on Stafford's syllabus for the workshop day itself.

**Key terms:** autonomous technology, technics-out-of-control, the technological imperative, reverse adaptation; apparatus, technique, organization, network (the four senses of "technology" in the introduction, pp. 11–12, per Anderson's reading notes, seen).
