# A Precarious Game: The Illusion of Dream Jobs in the Video Game Industry

**Citation:** Bulut, Ergin. *A Precarious Game: The Illusion of Dream Jobs in the Video Game Industry*. Ithaca, NY: ILR Press, an imprint of Cornell University Press, 2020. https://doi.org/10.1515/9781501746543.

**What it argues:** An ethnography of almost three years inside a medium-sized U.S. studio (pseudonymously "Desire," owned by a publisher Bulut calls Digital Creatives) asks "For Whom the Love Works" (introduction title, seen on MUSE). The **dream job** of making games rests on an **unequal ludopolitical regime** (chapter 1): developers' passion depends on spouses' domestic labor, on testers treated as "precarious second-class citizens" (chapter 6), and on racialized and gendered workers in the Global South, and the rhetoric of loving the work disguises those inequalities and blocks collective action (MUSE description and Woodcock's review, seen). The book ends with layoffs reaching the "core creatives" (chapter 7).

**Why it's canonical:** It is the field's standard ethnography of game-studio labor, the fieldwork counterpart to *Games of Empire*'s political economy, reviewed across labor, communication, and Marxist journals and open access, so it is easy to assign.

**Key terms:** precarious labor, ludopolitical regime, dream jobs, love of work, technomasculinity, degradation of fun (testers), the affective studio.
