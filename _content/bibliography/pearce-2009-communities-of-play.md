# Communities of Play

**Citation:** Pearce, Celia, and Artemesia. *Communities of Play: Emergent Cultures in Multiplayer Games and Virtual Worlds*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2009. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/8039.001.0001. (Artemesia is Pearce's avatar, credited as co-author.)

**What it argues:** An ethnography of the Uru Diaspora, players (mostly baby boomers) whose game, *Uru: Ages Beyond Myst*, closed; calling themselves refugees, they moved into other worlds, above all There.com, where they built a hybrid culture carrying their old world with them and went from outsiders to community leaders. Pearce reads this as **emergent culture**, what players do that the designers did not intend, looks at how a world's design shapes it, and, against the ludological view of play as unproductive, argues that play can be a prelude to creativity (summary from the publisher's abstract, seen via OpenAlex; the book's text was not opened).

**Why it's canonical:** It is the classic study of a player community that outlives its game, assigned for the communities or multiplayer session of introductory courses (MIT 2011, Swarthmore), and a model for ethnography of virtual worlds written partly through the researcher's own avatar.

**Key terms:** emergent culture, the Uru Diaspora, play communities, intergame immigration, the "play turn," productive play, the avatar as co-author.

**For the game (TH proposal):** when the AI takes a node, the community that lived there does not vanish: its faction token moves to a neighboring node and brings one house rule with it, written into the ruleset in `/game/edit`, so the map's culture changes as people are displaced (B, editor).
