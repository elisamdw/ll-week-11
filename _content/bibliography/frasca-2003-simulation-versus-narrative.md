# Simulation versus Narrative: Introduction to Ludology

**Citation:** Frasca, Gonzalo. "Simulation versus Narrative: Introduction to Ludology." In *The Video Game Theory Reader*, edited by Mark J. P. Wolf and Bernard Perron, 221–235. New York: Routledge, 2003. (Page range unverified.)

**What it argues:** Games are not stories but **simulations**: "to simulate is to model a (source) system through a different system which maintains to somebody some of the behaviors of the original system" (preprint, seen). **Ludology**, the formal study of games, "does not disdain this dimension of video games but claims that they are not held together by a narrative structure" (preprint, seen). Simulations carry ideology at four levels: representation, **manipulation rules** (what players can do), **goal rules** (what they must do to win), and **meta-rules**, "a rule that states how rules can be changed," as in games that ship with editors (preprint, seen). He borrows Caillois's **paidia** and **ludus** to separate open play from rule-bound games.

**Why it's canonical:** It is the essay that named and defined ludology for a general reader in the first *Video Game Theory Reader*, and it is assigned as the ludology side of the ludology/narratology debate (MIT CMS.300, Swarthmore FMST 86).

**Key terms:** ludology, simulation, simauthor, manipulation rules, goal rules, meta-rules, paidia and ludus, the four ideological levels of a simulation.

**For the game (TH proposal):** the `/game/edit` editor is Frasca's fourth level; each rule's "rhetoric" field names which level it works at (representation, manipulation, goal, or meta), and one meta-rule says how many rules a faction may change per game (editor).
