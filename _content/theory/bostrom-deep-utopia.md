# Deep Utopia: Life and Meaning in a Solved World

Nick Bostrom's sequel to *Superintelligence* (Ideapress Publishing, 27 March 2024; 525–536 pages depending on the listing) asks what people would do with their lives if the AI transition goes well, told as a week of fictional lectures by an older Bostrom, with handouts, assigned stories, and student interruptions.

## The argument

*Superintelligence* asked how things go wrong; this book asks "But what if things go right?" (publisher page). Bostrom imagines a **solved world** at technological maturity, a **post-instrumental** condition in which "human efforts are not needed for any practical purpose," and where "human nature itself becomes fully malleable" (publisher page, seen). Reviewers report his terms for that malleability as **plasticity** and **autopotency**, the ability to edit our own bodies and mental states (Chace 2024), and a split between **shallow redundancy** (machines do our jobs) and **deep redundancy** (no task, even a pastime, is worth our effort because a machine or a self-edit does it better). Bostrom treats shallow redundancy as easy, a leisure problem, and spends most of the book on the deep one: the **purpose problem**. His answer is a layered defence of meaning (reviewers call it "five rings of defence"; The Independent 2024), whose fourth layer is **artificial purpose**: goals we set ourselves, with rules that forbid the shortcuts, as games do. That is where the book meets the philosophy of games: in a solved world, a game is the activity that survives because its obstacles are chosen. In the Clearer Thinking interview he uses golf as the example, which is Bernard Suits's own example in *The Grasshopper* (1978); for Suits's definition see RS's entry in `NB/_context/projects/50-in-focus/ll-weeks/fall-2026/11-ending-20261120/research/learning-games-classics.md`.

## Critiques and conversations

- Hammerton (NDPR 2024): Bostrom raises the motivating concerns early and "unfortunately never circles back"; "some readers may be disappointed that he doesn't push towards more definite answers" (seen).
- Tibor Fischer, *The Spectator* (18 June 2024): "As an experiment, it's admirably unique, bonkers and daring, but I don't feel it works" (seen). Kirkus (17 May 2024) is warmer: "A complex and stimulatingly provocative look at just how possible a fulfilling life might be" (seen).
- Scott Alexander: "this book was crying out for fictional stories set in Deep Utopia" (seen). Ginevra Davis (*Arena*, 3 June 2024) notes that Bostrom "never explores how anything other than humans could fare" at technological maturity (seen).
- Bernard Suits's utopia of games is the older version of this argument; see RS's Suits entry (path above) and the debate over whether Suits's games are possible in a world with no scarcity (Yorke, Hurka; not opened).

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (A, C) **The game's last phase is the solved world:** if the humans keep the AI in check, the closing round asks each faction to name what it will still do when the AI can do everything, scored by the table; the debrief question is Bostrom's, "what is the point?"
- (B) **Artificial purpose as a human resource:** factions can play "self-imposed rule" cards (no AI help on this node) that cost efficiency but score meaning points, Suits's "less efficient means" as a mechanic.
- (C) **Two clocks, two endings:** the AI team's incremental screen ends in paperclips, the humans' map can end in deep utopia; the debrief compares which ending each side found more boring.
- (A, B) **Shallow vs deep redundancy as an event deck:** early events take jobs (shallow), late events take hobbies and crafts (deep), so players feel the shift Bostrom describes.
