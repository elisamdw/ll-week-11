# Half-Real

A book-length theory of video games (MIT Press, 2005) by the Danish game scholar Jesper Juul, followed by his short book on losing, *The Art of Failure: An Essay on the Pain of Playing Video Games* (MIT Press, 2013).

## The argument

A video game is **half-real**: its **rules** exist in the world (we interact with them, and winning or losing happens to us, not to a character) while its **fiction** is imagined ("we slay a dragon (for example) only in the world of the game"). The rules decide what the game does; the fiction tells the player how to read the rules, and the two often disagree. Juul's **classic game model** names six features shared by games for millennia: rules; variable, quantifiable outcome; valorization of outcomes; player effort; player attached to outcome; negotiable consequences. He sorts challenge into two structures: **emergence** ("a number of simple rules combining to form interesting variations," the structure of board, strategy, and card games) and **progression** ("separate challenges presented serially," as in adventure games, which hands the designer control of the sequence). *The Art of Failure* adds the **paradox of failure**: we avoid failure in life but seek it in games, because failure "helps us reconsider our strategies" and because games can produce small tragedies of our own making. This matters for a game in which the humans may be meant to lose to the AI.

- *Half-Real*, Introduction (pp. 1–22, about 40 minutes), free as a PDF on the book's site (https://half-real.net/sample/half-real-introduction.pdf): half-real, emergence vs progression, and the book's map.

## Passages

"To play a video game is therefore to interact with real rules while imagining a fictional world, and a video game is a set of rules as well as a fictional world." (*Half-Real*, Introduction, p. 1) (seen)

"A classic game is a rule-based formal system with a variable and quantifiable outcome, where different outcomes are assigned different values, the player exerts effort in order to influence the outcome, the player feels attached to the outcome, and the consequences of the activity are optional and negotiable." ("The Game, the Player, the World," 2003, https://jesperjuul.net/text/gameplayerworld/) (seen)

"1. We generally avoid failure. 2. We experience failure when playing games. 3. We seek out games, although we will experience something that we normally avoid." (*The Art of Failure*, ch. 1, Salon excerpt) (seen)

## For the game (TH proposal)

- **Rules and fiction in separate fields (B, editor):** the JSON ruleset keeps numbers (`rules`) and names and flavour text (`fiction`) apart, so a group can change one without the other; this is Juul's half-real made into the editor's structure.
- **Emergence, not script (B):** the AI deck should be a few simple rules that combine (spread to the cheapest adjacent energy, then to water, then to whatever is coupled) rather than a sequence of scripted events; the hidden "turn" card is the one deliberate piece of progression.
- **Losing is the lesson (B, debrief):** set the default ruleset so the humans usually lose the first game, and make the debrief the "second play" where the class reconsiders its strategy (Juul's paradox of failure); a win becomes possible only after a rule change.
- **Negotiable consequences check (debrief, all):** ask what in the game had consequences outside it (grades, friendships, a lingering argument about AI), which is Juul's sixth feature tested on a game about the world's energy.
