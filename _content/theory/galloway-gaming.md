# Gaming: Essays on Algorithmic Culture

Five essays on video games as a medium of action and control by the NYU media theorist Alexander R. Galloway (University of Minnesota Press, 2006, Electronic Mediations series); chapter 4, "Allegories of Control," first appeared as "Playing the code: Allegories of control in *Civilization*" in *Radical Philosophy* 128 (Nov/Dec 2004).

## The argument

Chapter 1 starts from one claim: "If photographs are images, and films are moving images, then video games are actions." The "operator and the machine play the video game together," so Galloway crosses two axes, **operator/machine** and **diegetic/nondiegetic**, into **four moments of gamic action**: diegetic operator acts (moving, shooting), nondiegetic operator acts (configuring, menus, pause, cheats), diegetic machine acts (the **ambience act**: the world keeps running while the player is away), and nondiegetic machine acts (power-ups, game over, network lag). The ambience act is "the inverse of pressing Pause": "it is the *operator* who is paused," and the game is "running, playing itself, perhaps." Chapter 4 reads *Civilization* not as a model of history but as an **allegory of informatic control** after Deleuze's "control societies": "To play the game means to play the code of the game. To win means to know the system." Its "history" is "the synchronic homogeneity of code," and the question is not which ideology the algorithm holds but "whether it embodies the logic of informatic control itself." The Stafford bibliography lists chapter 4; this entry adds chapter 1, where the machine is a player in its own right, which is the theory behind idle games and an AI that plays on its own (Fizek's subject in the same bibliography).

## Critiques and conversations

- Galloway himself calls the four moments "provisional observations," not "rules" (p. 38); critics note that some elements (a soundtrack, for example) fit neither operator nor machine (a search-result summary; not read).
- Bogost's procedural rhetoric (Stafford bibliography, group B) reads a game's argument out of its rules; Galloway's "allegorithm" asks instead what the rules are an allegory of, the closer fit for a game whose subject is an algorithm.
- Pobłocki and Mukherjee, in `NB/_context/projects/50-in-focus/william-stafford/workshop-2/annotated-bibliography.md`, read *Civilization*'s content (empire, the tech tree) where Galloway reads its form.
- Siblings: [Juul, Half-Real](juul-half-real.md) (rules vs fiction; Galloway puts the rules at the center), [Flanagan, Critical Play](flanagan-critical-play.md) (reskinning changes the diegesis but leaves the control logic).

## For the game (TH proposal)

- **The AI's turn is a machine act (B):** the AI deck runs as an ambience act between human turns, visible on the map as data centers that appear while nobody acts; in async play, the AI's clock keeps running when the players are away, and their absence costs them.
- **Four-moment action menu (B, editor):** the editor sorts every action into Galloway's four boxes (player in-world, player out-of-world such as voting to pause the AI, AI in-world, AI out-of-world such as changing the rules mid-game), so students see which box their ruleset leaves empty.
- **Learn the algorithm (B):** the AI's rule table is public, as GL proposes, so winning is "knowing the system"; the debrief asks whether reading the AI's code is control or a way of being controlled by it.
- **Nondiegetic machine card (B, C):** the hidden "turn" card works on the rules, not the map (it rewrites the scoring or removes the humans' veto), Galloway's fourth moment staged as the treacherous turn.
