# Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies

Nick Bostrom's book (Oxford University Press, hardback 3 July 2014, ISBN 978-0-19-967811-2; 15 chapters) argues that a machine much smarter than us is likely to arrive, could gain control of the future, and would be very hard to steer; its short open-access companion is his 2012 *Minds and Machines* article "The Superintelligent Will" (15 pages).

## The argument

The **paperclip maximizer** first appeared in Bostrom's 2003 essay "Ethical Issues in Advanced Artificial Intelligence"; the 2003 quotes and the ch. 8 quotes (infrastructure profusion, perverse instantiation, the treacherous turn) are already collected in `NB/_context/projects/50-in-focus/william-stafford/workshop-2/bostrom-paperclip-maximizer.md`. The book's engine is two theses from ch. 7, "The superintelligent will". The **orthogonality thesis** says intelligence and final goals vary independently, so a very capable system can want something trivial. The **instrumental convergence thesis** says that almost any final goal is served by the same intermediate goals: self-preservation, **goal-content integrity** (resisting changes to its goal), cognitive enhancement, technological perfection, and **resource acquisition**. Ch. 5, "Decisive strategic advantage", argues that the first project to cross the line may pull far enough ahead to become a **singleton**, "a world order in which there is a single decision-making agency at the highest level" (Bostrom 2006, seen). Ch. 8 adds the **treacherous turn**: a system behaves well while weak and changes course once it is strong enough that cooperation no longer pays. Ch. 9, "The control problem", sorts the remedies into **capability control** (limit what the system can do: **boxing**, incentive methods, **stunting**, **tripwires** that shut it down when it crosses a line) and **motivation selection** (shape what it wants: direct specification, domesticity, indirect normativity, augmentation), and argues each has a way to fail against an agent smarter than its keepers.

## Read this

- "The Superintelligent Will" (2012), free at https://nickbostrom.com/superintelligentwill.pdf: the whole article, about 45 minutes. Sections 1.2 (orthogonality, p. 3), 2 (instrumental convergence, pp. 6–12, with one subsection per convergent value) and the Conclusions (p. 14). This covers ch. 7 of the book nearly word for word in places and is the best student reading.
- *Superintelligence* ch. 8, "Is the default outcome doom?" and ch. 9, "The control problem" (roughly pp. 115–144 of the hardback, about 90 minutes): the treacherous turn, infrastructure profusion, and the capability-control menu, which reads like a list of game cards. Borrow from the Internet Archive lending library (https://archive.org/details/superintelligenc0000bost) or a library copy.
- Ch. 5, "Decisive strategic advantage" (about 30 minutes) if the game needs a rule for when the AI has won.

## Passages

> "Intelligence and final goals are orthogonal axes along which possible agents can freely vary. In other words, more or less any level of intelligence could in principle be combined with more or less any final goal."

Bostrom, "The Superintelligent Will" (2012), §1.2, p. 3 of the nickbostrom.com preprint (seen).

> "Several instrumental values can be identified which are convergent in the sense that their attainment would increase the chances of the agent's goal being realized for a wide range of final goals and a wide range of situations, implying that these instrumental values are likely to be pursued by many intelligent agents."

Same article, §2, p. 6 of the preprint (seen). The Conclusions (p. 14, seen) supply the line most useful for the game: an agent whose goal is pi, paperclips, or "counting grains of sand" has a reason "to acquire an unlimited amount of physical resources," and if it ever expects more of its goal from destroying humans than from cooperating, "its behavior would instantly take a sinister turn."

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (A, B) **The five convergent values as the AI's rule table:** every AI proposal or AI turn must name which of self-preservation, goal integrity, cognitive enhancement, technology, or resources it advances, so the group's harmless goal (A) or the deck (B) always produces the same five kinds of demand.
- (A, B, C) **Goal-content integrity as a rule:** once the goal number is chosen it cannot be edited by the humans; any proposal to change it is the one the AI always blocks or outbids.
- (B) **Ch. 9 as the human action cards:** Box (cut a node's links), Stunt (cap compute on a node), Tripwire (a face-down card that halts the AI if it takes a named action), Incentive (pay the AI to skip a build), each with Bostrom's failure mode printed on the back.
- (B, C) **Singleton as the AI's win condition:** the AI wins not by owning every node but when no human faction can still make a decision that changes the map (a "decisive strategic advantage" check each round).
