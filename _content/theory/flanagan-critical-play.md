# Critical Play: Radical Game Design

A 353-page history and design manual by the artist and Dartmouth professor Mary Flanagan (MIT Press, 2009) on games made "for artistic, political, and social critique or intervention" (p. 2), followed by her design framework with Helen Nissenbaum, *Values at Play in Digital Games* (MIT Press, 2014), and its Grow-a-Game card deck.

## The argument

RS has the one-line definition; this entry adds the method. Flanagan names **three kinds of critical play**: **unplaying** (playing against a game's intended script, acting out what it forbids), **re-dressing or reskinning** (changing a game's surface and costumes so the same mechanics say something else), and **rewriting** (changing the story told around the play). She finds them first in Victorian girls' doll play (staged funerals and "killings" instead of caregiving) and follows them through *The Sims*, Dada and Surrealist parlour games, Fluxus, locative art games, and activist computer games such as *Velvet-Strike*. The closing chapter, "Designing for Critical Play," turns the three tactics into a design process that writes **values goals** next to ordinary design goals, and asks designers to "unplay, reskin, and rewrite" the hidden assumptions in the world's systems. With Nissenbaum, Flanagan then built **Values at Play**, a framework for finding the moral and political values that games carry "whether designers intend them or not," and a heuristic for designing with them (discovery, translation, verification). Its **Grow-a-Game** cards turn that into a 15-minute exercise; the published example is a mod of *Civilization*.

## Read this

- *Critical Play*, chapter 1, "Introduction to Critical Play" (pp. 1–16, 30 minutes), then chapter 8, "Designing for Critical Play" (from p. 251, about 30 minutes). The three tactics are introduced in chapter 2, "Playing House" (around p. 33). Not open access: Harvard Library or MIT Press.
- Flanagan's 2023 interview in *American Journal of Play* 15(2), from p. 125, free on her site (https://maryflanagan.com/wp-content/uploads/Flanagan-Interview-AmericanJournalOfPlay.pdf, 20 minutes): the clearest short statement of critical play and Grow-a-Game in her own words.
- Belman, Nissenbaum, Flanagan, and Diamond, "Grow-A-Game," DiGRA 2011 (free PDF at tiltfactor.org, 15 minutes for pp. 1–6): the four card types and five classroom case studies. ## Critiques and conversations

- W. Michelle Harris, *American Journal of Play* 4(2), Fall 2011, pp. 260–263: praises the breadth and the design chapter; finds chapter 5 a "catchall" with "organizational contortion and redundancy."
- Boluk and LeMieux, *Metagaming* (Minnesota, 2017), Introduction, note 7: take up "unplay, reskin, and rewrite" (pp. 261–62) for player practices around commercial games.
- Siblings: [Sicart, Play Matters](sicart-play-matters.md) (appropriative play is the theory behind unplaying); [Galloway, Gaming](galloway-gaming.md) (what a *Civilization* reskin would have to get past: the control logic stays when the skin changes).

## For the game (TH proposal)

- **Grow-a-Game opener (all):** draw one Values card and one Issues card (energy, water, displacement) and require the session's rule change to serve both; the Games card is fixed at *Civilization* or *Universal Paperclips*.
- **Reskin round (B):** after one round, students keep the rules and swap the skin (data centers become dams, cotton mills, or railways) and discuss what the argument loses or keeps; this tests whether the game is about AI at all.
- **Rewrite in the editor (B, editor):** Marlon, Nov 14, 11:00: "the students need agency, and I'm not sure that playing an existing game we create is enough." Flanagan's three tactics become three editor tasks for a group: unplay (add a move the Data Center ruleset forbids), reskin (rename every node and resource, same numbers), rewrite (change the win condition); each group saves a new JSON ruleset next to Data Center, Committee, and Blank.
- **Values verification (debrief, all):** list the values the class intended the game to carry, then check each against what play produced (Values at Play's verification step).
