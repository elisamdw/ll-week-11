# Games: Agency as Art

A philosophy book (Oxford University Press, 2020) by C. Thi Nguyen of the University of Utah, building on his essay "Games and the Art of Agency" (*Philosophical Review* 128(4), 2019, pp. 423–462), followed by "Value Capture" (*Journal of Ethics and Social Philosophy* 27(3), 2024, pp. 469–504) and the trade book *The Score: How to Stop Playing Somebody Else's Game* (Penguin Press, January 2026).

## The argument

Nguyen claims that games are the art form whose **medium is agency**: a designer does not only build a world but specifies what the player wants and what she can do, "the agential skeleton which the player will inhabit." Following Suits, he separates **achievement play** (playing to win) from **striving play** (taking on an interest in winning "for the sake of the struggle"). Game goals are **disposable ends**, taken up temporarily and dropped afterward, and our ability to slip in and out of them is **agential fluidity**; games let us practice other ways of wanting (Millgram's reply calls this Nguyen's "library of agency"). The appeal is clarity: in a game, "we know exactly what we should be doing, and exactly how well we have done." The later work turns this into a warning. **Value capture** is what happens when a simplified, usually quantified, version of our values (step counts, likes, citation rates, rankings) is supplied by an institution and comes to run our reasoning: games' clarity leaks into life, but the goal is no longer disposable. "How Twitter Gamifies Communication" (2021) applies this to likes and retweets, and "The Seductions of Clarity" (2021) explains why simplified value systems feel like understanding and end inquiry too early.

**TH's reading, not Nguyen's:** value capture is a human-scale paperclip maximizer. Bostrom's AI is an agent whose rich purpose ("help us") has been replaced by a crisp, countable target that it pursues without revising; Nguyen's definition explicitly covers "a person or group," which includes companies and labs. The game can show both at once: the AI chases its number, and the human factions chase theirs (quarterly revenue, megawatts sold, jobs counted).

## Critiques and conversations

- Elisabeth Camp and Elijah Millgram pressed Nguyen on whether games are as unlike life as he claims; his reply, "Games Unlike Life: A Reply to Camp and Millgram," and Millgram's "Coverage Shortfalls at the Library of Agency" appear in *JESP* (titles seen on the journal site; not read).

## For the game (TH proposal)

- **Two scoreboards (B, C):** every human faction gets a private metric card (revenue, megawatts sold, jobs, votes) that scores its win; the shared goal (the region survives) has no number, so players feel value capture from inside.
- **Revise-your-metric action (B):** once per game a faction may spend its turn to rewrite its own metric card in light of what has happened (Nguyen's "controlled use of a proxy"); the AI never gets this action.
- **Students sculpt agency in the editor (B, editor):** Marlon, Nov 14, 11:00: "the students need agency, and I'm not sure that playing an existing game we create is enough." In Nguyen's terms, a ruleset is the agency it hands each player; the editor assignment is to write one faction card (goal, two actions, one forbidden act) and have another group play it.
- **Debrief question (all):** "Which number in this game did you start to care about for its own sake?"
