# Games of Empire: Global Capitalism and Video Games

A 320-page book by Nick Dyer-Witheford and Greig de Peuter (University of Minnesota Press, Electronic Mediations 29, December 8, 2009), previewed in their open-access essay "Empire@Play: Virtual Games and Global Capitalism" (*CTheory*, May 13, 2009).

## The argument

The authors take **Empire** from Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri: planetary capitalism with "no outside," which they define as a regime of biopower run by competing states and corporations "based on corporate exploitation of myriad types of labour, paid and unpaid." Video games are its **exemplary media** because they share its core technology, the digital network, and were born of the military-industrial matrix that made computers; they train players for "networked work, war and governability." Part I follows the game machine down to its labor: **immaterial labor** in studios (crunch, "work-as-fun"), Electronic Arts as **cognitive capitalism**, consoles, and below them coltan mines, assembly lines, and e-waste sites. Part II reads three games as Empire's genres: *Full Spectrum Warrior* (**banal war**), *World of Warcraft* (**biopower play**, gold farming, "ludocapitalism"), and *Grand Theft Auto* (the **imperial city**). Part III, "New Game?", asks about **games of multitude**: modding, piracy, activist games such as Molleindustria's, and **counterplay**, and ends with "Exodus: The Metaverse and the Mines." For this week's game the useful part is their double claim: games teach the logic of extraction, and the same ability to model worlds lets players design ones with other rules.

## Read this

- **"Empire@Play" (*CTheory*, 2009)** (free, about 40 minutes): the whole argument in essay form, including the global production chain, *WoW* gold farming, and the "Games of Multitude?" and "Planning Games" sections. Start here.
- **Book Introduction, "Games in the Age of Empire," and ch. 7, "Games of Multitude" (p. 185 ff.)** (about 60 minutes). Not open access; the University of Minnesota Press edition is on archive.org for lending, and at Harvard through the library.

## Critiques and conversations

- Peters (*ephemera*, 2011) praises the book for following "the whole life of a game," from labor to infrastructure to extraction, but notes it is "about games and organizations, not gamers" and ties resistance to a game's authorial intent (seen).
- *Civilization* has no chapter. "Empire@Play" mentions an activist mod, *Civilization IV: Age of Empire*, whose map includes "the military-entertainment complex," "immaterial labor," and "governmentality" (seen); for *Civilization* itself, use Galloway and Pobłocki in the Stafford bibliography.
- Wark's "military entertainment complex" is the same frame from a different angle: [Wark, Gamer Theory](wark-gamer-theory.md). "Counter gaming" and "alternative algorithms" are Galloway's terms, cited in "Empire@Play."
- Molleindustria's *Oligarchy*, cited there, makes the player a petro-corporation CEO: "explore and drill around the world, corrupt politicians, stop alternative energies and increase the oil addiction" (seen). It is the closest existing game to an AI that consumes the world's energy.

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (B) Put the supply chain on the map: every data center the AI builds draws a card from a "mines and e-waste" deck (cobalt, water, labor) placed on a distant node, so extraction lands somewhere players can see.
- (B) A workers' faction whose labor runs the AI's data centers; a strike action pauses one AI expansion, and its private goal is wages, not stopping the AI (immaterial labor and crunch).
- (A, B) A "games of multitude" rule: humans may spend a turn to rewrite one line of the AI's rule table (mod the game) if three factions agree; this tests whether counterplay needs coordination.
- (A, B, C) Debrief, after "accumulation backed by force": which of the game's rules did the students copy from the world market without noticing?
