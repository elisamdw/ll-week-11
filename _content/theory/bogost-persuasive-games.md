# Persuasive Games: The Expressive Power of Videogames

Ian Bogost's book on how videogames argue through their rules (MIT Press, 2007, about 450 pages), condensed by Bogost himself into a 24-page open-access chapter, "The Rhetoric of Video Games," in Katie Salen's MacArthur volume *The Ecology of Games* (MIT Press, 2008, pp. 117–140).

## The argument

Bogost says games represent systems by building rules, which he calls **procedurality**, and that rules can be authored to persuade, which he calls **procedural rhetoric**: an argument made "not through the construction of words or images, but through the authorship of rules of behavior" (2008, p. 125). Rules create a **possibility space**, the set of moves they allow, and play is how a player explores that space and discovers the argument inside it. His running examples: *Animal Crossing*, where paying off Tom Nook's mortgage only leads to a bigger house and a bigger loan, so the game models debt and acquisition without preaching; and Molleindustria's *The McDonald's Videogame*, an **anti-advergame** in which the player can only succeed long-term by bribing, bulldozing, and using growth hormones, so the rules argue that corruption is built into the business. The book sorts persuasive games into **three domains**, politics, advertising, and learning (its three parts), and ends the learning part with **procedural literacy**: learning to read, critique, and write arguments in rules. For a course on infrastructure, the point is that a rule set, like a database schema, argues by what it allows, rewards, and forbids. RS's summary paragraph and the book's definition ("the art of persuasion through rule-based representations and interactions," p. ix) are in `NB/_context/projects/50-in-focus/ll-weeks/fall-2026/11-ending-20261120/research/learning-games-classics.md`; this entry adds the passages.

## Passages

> "I suggest the name procedural rhetoric for the practice of using processes persuasively, just as verbal rhetoric is the practice of using oratory persuasively and visual rhetoric is the practice of using images persuasively."

"The Rhetoric of Video Games," in *The Ecology of Games* (MIT Press, 2008), p. 125 (seen).

> "The McDonald's Videogame mounts a procedural rhetoric about the necessity of corruption in the global fast food business, and the overwhelming temptation of greed, which leads to more corruption. In order to succeed in the long-term, the player must use growth hormones, he must coerce banana republics, and he must mount PR and lobbying campaigns."

Same chapter, p. 127 (seen). On *Animal Crossing* (p. 119, seen): "Animal Crossing accomplishes this feat not through moralistic regulation, but by creating a model of commerce and debt in which the player can experience and discover such consequences."

## Critiques and conversations

- Miguel Sicart, "Against Procedurality" (*Game Studies* 11.3, 2011): meaning comes from play, not rules alone. Covered in RS's file (path above) and in [Sicart, Play Matters](sicart-play-matters.md).
- Bogost's earlier theory of how simulations leave a gap that players fill (the **simulation gap**, **simulation fever**) is in [Bogost, Unit Operations](bogost-unit-operations.md), not in this book.
- Gonzalo Frasca's *September 12th* (Newsgaming, 2003), a game you cannot win by bombing, is the usual classroom example of procedural rhetoric; whether and where Bogost discusses it in the book was not confirmed.
- His later critique of gamification as practice: [Bogost, Cow Clicker](bogost-cow-clicker.md).

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (A, B, C) A closing debrief card: each player states the game's argument in one sentence using only what the rules made them do, then compares it with what the designers meant.
- (B) An anti-advergame scoring rule, as in *The McDonald's Videogame*: the company faction's most profitable action (selling power and water to the AI) is the same action that harms everyone, so the argument sits in the score table.
- (A) A Tom Nook loop: each proposal the committee approves unlocks a larger next proposal, shown on a "commitments" track, so every yes creates the demand for the next one.
- (B) A "rhetoric" field for every rule in `rules.json`: students who edit a rule must write the claim their change makes about AI infrastructure.
