# Critiques of the Superintelligence picture

Five well-sourced replies to Bostrom's [*Superintelligence*](bostrom-superintelligence.md) argument, from a 2016 conference talk to a 2026 philosophy preprint, each aimed at a different link in the chain.

## The argument

Bostrom's case is a chain: intelligence can grow fast (the **singularity** or intelligence-explosion premise); a highly intelligent system can have any goal (**orthogonality**); almost any goal pushes it toward power and resources (**instrumental convergence**); so the default outcome is a system that takes over. The critics below each cut a different link. Mitchell denies orthogonality: in every mind we know, intelligence and values grow together. Pinker and Zador and LeCun accept that goals are separate from intelligence (close to orthogonality) but deny that a drive to dominate follows; their quarrel is with instrumental convergence, and they read the fear as human psychology projected onto machines. Thorstad, a philosopher formerly at Oxford's Global Priorities Institute, attacks the growth premise and then the strength of instrumental convergence. Cegłowski attacks the whole frame: its slippery definition of intelligence, its resemblance to religion, and its cost in attention drawn away from present harms. A sixth line, the **TESCREAL** critique by Timnit Gebru and Émile P. Torres, treats the ideology around the book rather than its argument; see the sibling entry [TESCREAL](tescreal.md).

## The critics

### Maciej Cegłowski, "Superintelligence: The Idea That Eats Smart People" (Web Camp Zagreb, 29 Oct 2016)

A talk written in reply to Bostrom's book, in two halves: eleven "inside view" arguments with titles like "The Argument From Wooly Definitions" and "The Argument From Complex Motivations", then outside-view arguments about what the belief does to the people who hold it. On orthogonality he writes: "AI alarmists believe in something called the Orthogonality Thesis. This says that even very complex beings can have simple motivations, like the paper-clip maximizer." His point that matters most for the game:

> "The pressing ethical questions in machine learning are not about machines becoming self-aware and taking over the world"

https://idlewords.com/talks/superintelligence.htm (seen; sentence continues on the page)

### Melanie Mitchell, "What Does It Mean to Align AI With Human Values?" (Quanta Magazine, 13 Dec 2022)

Mitchell, a complexity scientist at the Santa Fe Institute, quotes Bostrom's orthogonality thesis and denies its premise of a pure intelligence that can be bolted onto any goal: in humans, she writes, "intelligence is deeply interconnected with our goals and values."

> "Nothing in the current science of psychology or neuroscience supports this possibility."

https://www.quantamagazine.org/what-does-it-mean-to-align-ai-with-human-values-20221213/ (seen)

### Steven Pinker, "Thinking Does Not Imply Subjugating" (Edge Annual Question 2015)

> "AI dystopias project a parochial alpha-male psychology onto the concept of intelligence. They assume that superhumanly intelligent robots would develop goals like deposing their masters or taking over the world. But intelligence is the ability to deploy novel means to attain a goal; the goals are extraneous to the intelligence itself."

https://www.edge.org/response-detail/26243 (seen). Note that the last sentence agrees with orthogonality; Pinker's target is the jump from intelligence to conquest.

### Anthony Zador and Yann LeCun, "Don't Fear the Terminator" (Scientific American blog, 26 Sept 2019)

A neuroscientist and Meta's then chief AI scientist argue that the drive to dominate comes from evolution, not from intelligence.

> "But intelligence per se does not generate the drive for domination, any more than horns do."

https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/observations/dont-fear-the-terminator/ (seen). Bostrom's answer, in advance, is the 2012 article's §2: self-preservation and resource acquisition arise as means, not as drives.

### David Thorstad, "Against the singularity hypothesis" (Philosophical Studies 182 (7), 2025: 1627–1651; online 10 May 2024) and "Instrumental convergence and power-seeking" (arXiv, June 2026)

The 2024 paper examines Chalmers (2010) and Bostrom (2014) and finds their case for runaway self-improvement too weak; the 2026 paper argues that the power-seeking worry needs a strong form of instrumental convergence that no current defence, including the formal power-seeking theorems, establishes.

> "In this paper, I argue that the singularity hypothesis rests on undersupported growth assumptions."

Abstract, https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:20306911-5108-4c67-8288-f57a1a618a03 (seen). The 2026 abstract: he argues "that none establishes the thesis in a strong enough form to ground the argument from power-seeking" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08832, seen).

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (B, C) **Make the AI's rule table a contested object:** the AI's spread rules sit in the editable data file, and before play the table votes on Bostrom's settings (does the AI always seek resources?) or Thorstad's (convergence holds only some rounds), so the critique becomes a parameter.
- (B) **Cegłowski's present-harms track:** a second scoring track for harms that happen with no rogue AI at all (water bills, layoffs, outages from tight coupling); the humans can lose on this track while winning against the AI.
- (A) **Pinker's objection as a committee role:** one committee member holds a "goals are extraneous" card and may veto any proposal whose only justification is "the AI would want power," forcing the AI player to show the instrumental chain.
- (A, B, C) **Debrief question from Mitchell:** "Where in the game did the AI's intelligence and its goal come apart, and could they in any mind you know?"
