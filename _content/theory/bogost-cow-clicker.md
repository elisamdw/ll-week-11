# Cow Clicker, and gamification critique as practice

*Cow Clicker* was a Facebook game Ian Bogost launched in July 2010 to satirize FarmVille-style social games; it ran until the "Cowpocalypse" of September 7, 2011, and its record is Bogost's project page and launch essay on bogost.com and Jason Tanz's *Wired* feature (Dec. 20, 2011).

## The argument

Bogost made the game for Jesper Juul's NYU seminar "Social Games on Trial," as "an example that would act as its own theory," a practice he calls **carpentry**. The whole game: "You get a cow. You can click on it. In six hours, you can click it again." Clicks earn clicks, "mooney" buys premium cows or skips the wait, and clicking a friend's cow earns credit (bogost.com). His launch essay, "Cow Clicker: The Making of Obsession," names four things that troubled him in social games: **enframing** (friends turned into resources), **compulsion**, **optionalism** (play you can skip by paying), and **destroyed time**. The satire caught on: Tanz reports 50,000 players by early September 2010 and a peak of 56,000 that October, and that many stayed to play in earnest. Bogost extended the joke to gamification with **Cowclickification** (January 2011: an API to make any button a cow click), then set a countdown that players could delay by paying, and at the **Cowpocalypse** all the cows vanished, leaving clickable grass. Read with his 2011 Gamasutra column "Exploitationware," the point is that a game reduced to timers, points, and purchases treats players and their friends as inputs, and that the people who make such games get caught in the same loop.

## Passages

> "Social games are games you don't have to play."

"Cow Clicker: The Making of Obsession" (2010), https://bogost.com/blog/cow_clicker_1/ (seen). Also from it: "Such is the spirit of the day, it would seem: mundane, outward obsession whose worst trick is to disguise itself as fruitfulness." (seen)

> "In particular, gamification proposes to replace real incentives with fictional ones. Real incentives come at a cost but provide value for both parties based on a relationship of trust. By contrast, pretend incentives reduce or eliminate costs, but in so doing they strip away both value and trust."

"Persuasive Games: Exploitationware," Gamasutra, May 3, 2011 (seen). In the same column Bogost credits Margaret Robertson's "pointsification": gamification "takes the least essential aspects of games and presents them as the most essential" (Bogost's paraphrase of Robertson, seen). After the Cowpocalypse, a player told *Wired*: "It is very interesting, clicking nothing ... But then, we were clicking nothing the whole time. It just looked like we were clicking cows." (Tanz, 2011, seen)

## Critiques and conversations

- RS's file has the 2011 "Gamification is Bullshit" quote and Deterding et al.'s definition: `NB/_context/projects/50-in-focus/ll-weeks/fall-2026/11-ending-20261120/research/learning-games-classics.md`. Not repeated here.
- Players answered that they made their own fun inside the limits (Tanz quotes the top of the leaderboard, a UC Santa Barbara professor: "There is a fair amount of strategy—maybe more than Ian intended"); Bogost's reply, the essay "Shit Crayons," compares that creativity to a prisoner writing with whatever is at hand (as summarized by Tanz).
- *Cookie Clicker* (2013) and *Universal Paperclips* (2017) took the clicker form Bogost mocked and made it a genre; Lantz's game is the direct ancestor of this week's game. Bogost's later view of the work: "Feeling Herd," *The Atlantic*, 2022 (listed on bogost.com, not opened).
- The positive alternative he proposes, finding play in the thing itself: [Bogost, Play Anything](bogost-play-anything.md).

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (C) A Cow Clicker check for the AI screen: if it is fun only through timers, purchases, and a number going up, it is the thing Bogost satirized; show that loop on the screen, with a "stop now" button that stays available.
- (A, B, C) A Cowpocalypse clock: a public countdown to the AI's endgame that any player can delay by paying resources, so the price of postponing is visible and shared.
- (B) An exploitationware card for the company faction: its private goal pays off when it gives workers "fictional incentives" (points, badges) in place of wages or water; the debrief asks who paid.
- (A, B) After the game ends, leave the board open with the AI's object gone and count who keeps clicking: a debrief on optimizing a number after its purpose is gone, like Paperclips' ending.
