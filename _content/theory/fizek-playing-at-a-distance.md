# Playing at a Distance: Borderlands of Video Game Aesthetic

Sonia Fizek's open-access book (MIT Press, 2022, CC BY-NC-ND; about 125 pages of text plus notes) on games that play themselves, together with her earlier article "Interpassivity and the Joy of Delegated Play in Idle Games" (*Transactions of the Digital Games Research Association* 3, no. 3, April 2018, pp. 137–163).

## The argument

Fizek argues that interactivity, the idea that a game is a human making meaningful choices, no longer describes much of what players do: in idle games, auto battlers, bots, and streams, the machine acts and the player watches, sets parameters, and comes back later. She calls this **play at a distance** or **dis-play**: delegating the immediate action to the machine and taking part in the "(algorithmic) spectacle" instead. The 2018 article borrows **interpassivity** from Robert Pfaller and Slavoj Žižek (enjoyment delegated to someone or something else, like canned laughter) and calls idle games the first genre built on **delegated play**. The book's chapters each take one mode: interpassive, ambient, automated, intra-active (via Karen Barad), and spectated play. *Universal Paperclips* is one of her examples twice over: an "algorithmic spectacle" of numbers going up in the prelude, and in chapter 4 the moment the game takes over wire purchasing and runs on its own. Her larger point is that this "automatic turn" in games mirrors how we already live with automation and algorithmic agency, and she closes by noting that the ethics of delegated play, which she leaves mostly unexplored, "remain high."

The spelling is **Sonia** on the book's title page, its Library of Congress record, and the 2018 article. The Stafford bibliography (`NB/_context/projects/50-in-focus/william-stafford/workshop-2/annotated-bibliography.md`) says "William wrote 'Sonia'; the spelling is Sonja"; that note is wrong and William's spelling is right.

## Read this

- **Prelude, "Play at a Distance," pp. ix–xxi** (about 20 minutes): the definition of dis-play, the *Paperclips* mention, and the chapter overview. This is the reading already on the Stafford list.
- **Chapter 4, "Automated Play," pp. 51–66** (about 30 minutes): history of bots and automation in games, *Paperclips* (pp. 60–61), auto battlers, and the shift from "utter absorption" to "distracted habituation." The closest chapter to an AI-runs-the-world game.
- **The 2018 article, especially "Interpassive Games and Delegated Play," pp. 151 ff.** (about 30 minutes; free PDF at todigra.org). The book is free at MIT Press Direct (DOI 10.7551/mitpress/13605.001.0001) and as a single PDF on mediarep.org.

## Passages

> "And so, to dis-play is to be at a distance from an active and direct moment of play (from the Latin dis-, away, apart), to delegate the immediate action towards the machine, participating in the (algorithmic) spectacle (display) instead."

*Playing at a Distance*, Prelude, p. xvii (MIT Press OA PDF) (seen).

> "After producing over 62,000 paperclips, I gained the ability to delegate wire purchasing to an algorithm. From that moment on, the game was able to run fully autonomously, leaving the higher-level decision making to me."

*Playing at a Distance*, ch. 4, p. 61 (seen). On p. 62 she generalizes: "Automation transforms play from an act of 'utter absorption' to one of 'distracted habituation,' and the human player from a focused agent or attentive watcher to a casually observant spectator, to expand on Walter Benjamin." (seen) And from the 2018 article: "Idle games, discussed throughout this paper, seem to be the first video game genre to epitomise the delegation of pleasure and embody the interpassive relationship between players and the game." (ToDiGRA 3.3, p. 151, seen)

## Critiques and conversations

- Fizek's 2018 article lists "elimination of drudgery by automating and/or delegating all the laborious and repetitive in-game activities" as an idle-game trait and quotes Bogost on routine play "skipped through delegation" (p. 147); see [Bogost, Cow Clicker](bogost-cow-clicker.md) for the opposing view that such games train compulsion.
- She sets the book against the gatekeeping debate over which games count (Mia Consalvo and Christopher A. Paul's 2019 book) and alongside posthuman game studies (Alenda Chang, Brendan Keogh, Darshana Jayemanne) (Prelude, pp. xvii–xviii).
- Pair with [Lantz](lantz-games-thinking-about-thinking.md). Adam Rogers's *Wired* piece on *Paperclips* reads it the other way: "The game isn't fire-and-forget ... It's optimizable." The game sits between watching and tuning.

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (C) An "automate this" upgrade on the AI screen: each human-run task the AI team buys back removes a click and a decision from them, and at full automation the AI team can only watch and set priorities, as Fizek did at 62,000 clips.
- (B, C) A "come back later" turn for async play: the AI's rule table runs on a timer between sessions, and human players return, like Wark's *SimEarth* player, to whatever their absence allowed.
- (B) A delegation card for human factions: hand a node to an automated manager to save actions, at the cost of not seeing what it does until a failure surfaces (Perrow's tight coupling plus Fizek's distance).
- (A, B, C) Debrief question: at which moment did you stop playing and start watching, and who or what was playing then?
