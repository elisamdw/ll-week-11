# Play Matters

A short manifesto (158 pages plus notes) by the Copenhagen game scholar Miguel Sicart, published by MIT Press in 2014 in its Playful Thinking series, arguing that play, not games, is the thing worth studying.

## The argument

Sicart moves attention from the game as an object to **play as a mode of being in the world**: "Games are the finger; play is the moon." Play is **contextual**, **carnivalesque** (after Bakhtin: it mocks and overturns what it touches, producing laughter rather than fun), **appropriative** (it takes over its context and "cannot be totally predetermined by such context"), **disruptive**, **autotelic** but always *negotiated* (players keep renegotiating what the play is for), **creative**, and **personal**. Chapter 2 separates **play** (an activity) from **playfulness** (an attitude: carrying play's qualities into non-play contexts, such as playful interfaces). Chapters 3 and 4 treat **toys** (props that invite play) and **playgrounds** (spaces that afford a kind of play without imposing a purpose), and chapter 7 proposes the designer as an "architect of play" who makes the conditions for play rather than its meaning. For the game, the point is that whatever rules GL writes, students will appropriate them, and that appropriation is where the meaning gets made. This is the book-length version of the position Sicart staked out against Bogost in "Against Procedurality" (2011), which RS has already covered (see below).

## Read this

- Chapter 1, "Play Is" (pp. 1–18, about 30 minutes): the whole theory in compact form, including the one-move-a-day game *Vesper.5* (pp. 16–17), a model for an async game. Ask Harvard Library for the MIT Press edition; there is no open-access copy from the publisher.
- Chapter 2, "Playfulness," and chapter 7 on "architects of play" if there is time (about an hour together).
- *The Ethics of Computer Games* (MIT Press, 2009), only the introduction: Sicart's earlier argument that games are ethical objects and players ethical agents who bring their own judgment to a designed system. Kaplan's review in *Eludamos* 4(1), 2010, pp. 129–131, is a 3-page orientation.

## Critiques and conversations

- Michael DeAnda, *American Journal of Play* 7(2), Winter 2015, pp. 253–255: welcomes the challenge to the magic circle but says the theory "becomes problematic" when pushed, because Sicart does not set limits on "dark play" (p. 3) that shades into harassment.
- Sebastian Deterding's review in *Game Studies* 15(1), 2015, reads the book as a one-sided manifesto (not opened; the site was unreachable).
- RS's summary of "Against Procedurality" is in `NB/_context/projects/50-in-focus/ll-weeks/fall-2026/11-ending-20261120/research/learning-games-classics.md`; the Stafford bibliography lists it as optional.
- Siblings: [Flanagan, Critical Play](flanagan-critical-play.md) (subversive play as method), [Nguyen, Games: Agency as Art](nguyen-games-agency-as-art.md) (the opposite emphasis: the designer sculpts the player's agency).

## For the game (TH proposal)

- **Carnival card (B, C):** a human faction may play one "mockery" action per game (satire, protest art, a parody press release) that costs nothing on the map but raises public trust; asks whether laughter is a form of resistance to an optimizer.
- **One move a day (A, B):** the async version borrows *Vesper.5*'s rhythm (one action per player per day) so the game becomes a ritual that runs through the week, alongside the AI's own clock.
- **Appropriation goes into the file (B, editor):** Marlon, Nov 14, 11:00: "the students need agency, and I'm not sure that playing an existing game we create is enough." Sicart's appropriative play is the case for the editor: house rules that a table invents mid-game get saved as a new ruleset, so the next class plays the appropriated game, not ours.
- **Debrief question (all):** "Where did you stop playing our game and start playing your own?" Use the answers to revise the rules for William's class.
