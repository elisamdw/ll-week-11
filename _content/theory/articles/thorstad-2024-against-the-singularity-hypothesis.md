# Against the singularity hypothesis

David Thorstad (Vanderbilt University), a 25-page article in *Philosophical Studies* 182 (7), 2025, pp. 1627–1651, published online 10 May 2024. Licence, as printed on p. 1648: "Open Access This article is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License." The stored PDF is the publisher's version of record, from Oxford's ORA repository (Springer's own link refused automated download). **[The PDF](thorstad-2024-against-the-singularity-hypothesis.pdf)**.

## What it is

The strongest academic reply to the growth premise underneath Vinge, Chalmers, and Bostrom. Thorstad grants, for argument's sake, that general intelligence is a coherent idea and that machines may approach it, and attacks only the claim that self-improving AI will grow fast enough, for long enough, to leave us orders of magnitude behind. Section 3 gives **five reasons for skepticism**: extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence; **good ideas become harder to find** (the fishing-out argument); **bottlenecks** (one slow component limits the whole); **physical constraints** (energy, capital, materials, the end of Moore's law); and **sublinearity** (exponential gains in compute have bought at best linear gains in performance). Sections 4 and 5 test Chalmers's **proportionality thesis** and Bostrom's **optimization power over recalcitrance** model against them and find both short. Section 6 draws the consequence for doom arguments: without a fast takeoff, a single AI has a harder time becoming a singleton, and an argument for catastrophe has to show how ordinary capabilities would destroy humanity. For the game, section 3.4 is the useful surprise: the limits on the AI's growth are the same data centers, power plants, and supply chains on the B map.

## Segments

**What a singularity hypothesis says** (p. 1629):

> "First, it specifies a quantity. Second, it claims that this quantity will experience accelerated growth. And third, it claims that accelerated growth will lead to a discontinuity in human history."

**Bostrom's numbers** (p. 1631):

> "For example, on Bostrom’s (2014) model intelligence doubles in 7.5 months, grows a thousandfold within 17.9 months, and approaches infinity at 18 months."

**A forecast for 2026** (p. 1631), on extrapolating a curve:

> "One famous model from this period held that continued hyperbolic growth would lead to an infinite population by 2026 (Von Foerster et al., 1960). But it would have been a Malthusian mistake to project continued hyperbolic population growth."

**Fishing out** (p. 1634):

> "Suppose you are fishing without replacement from a deep pond. At first, catching fish is easy. The fish you catch dwell in shallow waters and are easily fooled into biting. But as time goes on, the time between catches grows longer."

**A feature of problems, not agents** (p. 1635):

> "However, the underlying problem of fishing-out is a feature of problems, not agents, and as such it cannot be eliminated by enlisting artificial agents as researchers."

**Bottlenecks** (p. 1635):

> "Growth processes often slow down because they hit bottlenecks: single obstacles which hinder further improvement until they can be overcome. Just one bottleneck is enough to slow growth."

**The infrastructure limit** (p. 1636):

> "Improving hardware requires mining key materials, constructing manufacturing plants, building capital equipment, and generating and distributing large amounts of energy. We may well be able to speed up these processes, but it is not plausible to expect them all to be made thousands of times more efficient overnight. And if even one process resists being sped up, the whole system of recursive self-improvement may be delayed."

**Exponential in, linear out** (p. 1637):

> "Across almost any metric we care to measure, sustained exponential growth in computer capacities has led at best to linear growth in performance."

**Don't ask** (p. 1638):

> "If, instead, the relevant notion of intelligence grows sublinearly in underlying computing capacities, then exponential growth in intelligence would require superexponential growth in underlying capacities. And hyperbolic growth? Don’t ask."

**Bostrom's formula** (pp. 1640–1641; the formula is set as a displayed fraction in the original):

> "With this understanding of optimization power and recalcitrance, intelligence grows fractionally in optimization power and recalcitrance: Rate of intelligence change = Optimization power / Recalcitrance."

**Time to react** (p. 1647):

> "As Bostrom acknowledges, slower emergence of superintelligence provides more time for other initiatives to compete for power, and more time for humanity to learn about and react to the growth of artificial systems."

**The burden for doom arguments** (p. 1647):

> "What this reminds us is that it is not enough to show that rogue artificial systems may cause financial disasters, manipulate humans, or misfire weapons. What needs to be demonstrated is that artificial systems will be powerful enough to destroy humanity or permanently curtail our potential. That is not an easy task."

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (B, editor) **Growth rate as a ruleset parameter:** the AI's compute-to-capability rule in `/game/edit` can be set to Bostrom (each data center makes the next one cheaper, hyperbolic) or Thorstad (each costs more than the last, sublinear); students play both and the rhetoric field names whose growth claim each encodes.
- (B) **Bottleneck nodes (pp. 1635–1636):** every AI upgrade needs one of each input (energy, chips, water, capital, labor), and the slowest supply on the map sets the AI's pace; humans win time by holding one input, not by fighting everywhere.
- (B) **Fishing-out deck:** the AI's research deck is sorted so the first upgrades are cheap and later ones cost more, and AI players cannot reshuffle it, because fishing-out is "a feature of problems, not agents."
- (debrief) **Which premise did the game assume?** After play, list the game's growth rule, its goal rule, and its power rule, and ask which of the three claims Thorstad finds in the Bostrom–Yudkowsky argument (the singularity hypothesis, orthogonality, instrumental convergence; §6.2, p. 1646) the table took for granted.
