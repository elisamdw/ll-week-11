# Playing the Code: Allegories of Control in Civilization

Alexander R. Galloway, an eight-page article in *Radical Philosophy* 128 (November/December 2004), pp. 33–40, later revised as the fourth chapter of *Gaming* (2006). Radical Philosophy posts the PDFs of issues 1–200 free on its archive site; the journal's page for the article gives the licence as Creative Commons BY-NC-ND 3.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/3.0/). **[The PDF](galloway-2004-playing-the-code.pdf)**.

## What it is

Galloway reads Sid Meier's *Civilization* with Fredric Jameson's allegorical criticism and Deleuze's "Postscript on Control Societies." Film, he says, hides the routine machinery of control (no one sees the booking and the indictment in a cop film); video games do the opposite and show it. Criticism of *Civilization* goes through three phases: doubt that games can be criticized at all, then **ideological critique** (expansionism, the black map that rewards exploring, *Civilization III*'s sixteen "civilizations" each tagged with two traits from a list of six, the tech tree's logocentrism), then a **formal critique**: the game embodies **informatic control** itself, through **flexibility** (sliders and coefficients that keep the system in balance) and **universal standardization** (everything made equivalent). The ideological reading then undoes itself: *Civilization*'s "history" is the absence of history. The closing claim: "now ideology is a decoy." The sibling entry already quotes "To play the game means to play the code of the game" and "they flaunt it" (both p. 35); the segments below are new.

## Segments

**Deleuze on control, as Galloway quotes him** (p. 34):

> "A control is not a discipline. In making freeways, for example, you donʼt enclose people but instead multiply the means of control. I am not saying that this is the freewayʼs exclusive purpose, but that people can drive infinitely and ʻfreelyʼ without being at all confined yet while still being perfectly controlled. This is our future."

**What should be on the screen** (p. 35):

> "This is unfortunate because, despite its unsexy screen presence, informatic control is precisely the most important thing to show on the screen, if one wishes to allegorize political power today."

**The player learns the algorithm** (p. 35):

> "In Meier, the gamer is not simply playing this or that historical simulation. The gamer is instead learning, internalizing and becoming intimate with a massive, multipartite global algorithm."

**Control as the whole game** (p. 35):

> "They solve the problem of political control, not by sublimating it as does the cinema, but by making it coterminous with the entire game. In this way video games achieve a unique type of political transparency."

**Buckminster Fuller's World Game, as Galloway quotes it** (p. 36):

> "In our World Game we propose to explore and test by assimilated adoption various schemes of ʻHow to Make the World Work.ʼ To win the World Game everybody must be made physically successful. Everybody must win."

**The map rewards expansion** (p. 37):

> "These conventions within both the narrative and the visual signification of the game therefore reward expansionism, even require it."

**Reification for the digital age** (pp. 37–38):

> "In short it transposes the many-layered quality of social life to an inflexible, reductive algorithm for ʻcivilizationʼ – a process not dissimilar to what Marxists used to call reification, only updated for the digital age. (The reason for doing this is of course a practical one: to create balanced gameplay, game designers require an array of variables that can be tweaked and tuned across the various environments and characters.)"

**Flexibility** (p. 38):

> "Flexibility is allegorically repurposed in Civilization via the use of various sliders and parameters to regulate flow and create systemic equilibrium. All elements in the game are put in quantitative, dynamic relationships with each other, such that a ʻCultural Victoryʼ conclusion of the game is differentiated from a ʻConquest Victoryʼ conclusion only through slight differences in the two algorithms for winning."

**History replaced by code** (p. 39):

> "So ʻhistoryʼ in Civilization is precisely the opposite of history, not because the game fetishizes the imperial perspective, but because the diachronic details of lived life are replaced by the synchronic homogeneity of code pure and simple."

**Ideology as decoy** (pp. 39–40):

> "In fact, the more emancipating games seem to be as a medium, substituting activity for passivity or a branching narrative for a linear one, the more they are in fact hiding the fundamental social transformation into informatics that has affected much of the globe during recent decades. In modernity, ideology was an instrument of power, but now ideology is a decoy, as I hope to have shown with the game Civilization."

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (B) **Fuller's win condition for the humans:** the human factions win only if every faction meets its private goal (p. 36, "Everybody must win"), which puts the cooperative game against the AI's single number in the rules themselves.
- (B, editor) **Show the sliders:** print the AI's coefficients (energy per data center, water per megawatt, spread rate) on a visible panel, and let students change them in `/game/edit`; the "rhetoric" field asks what the number flattens (pp. 37–38).
- (B) **Trait tags on the index cards:** if the students' infrastructure cards each get two tags from a short list (as *Civilization III* gives each civilization two of six traits, p. 37), the debrief asks what the tags left out of the place they describe.
- (debrief, all) **Where was the control?** After play, ask which rule controlled players while leaving them free to move (Deleuze's freeway, p. 34), and whether the AI won by force or by setting the terms.
