## What it is

Nguyen's account of how metrics change what people want. **Value capture** happens when an agent with rich or still-forming values meets a simplified, often quantified version of them in some institution, and that version "comes to dominate" their practical reasoning (p. 473). His cases are a Fitbit on a European holiday, the *US News* law school rankings (from Espeland and Sauder), and the UN's Human Development Index. He separates value capture from using a metric as a **proxy** under reflective control, and names a quieter form, **application capture**, where you still describe your values the old way but the metric decides each case. Why we let it happen: quantified data borrows trust through **cognitive fluency**, and metrics give us the **value clarity** of games in ordinary life, his link to *Games: Agency as Art*. The last sections turn from outside institutions to scale itself (**value swamping**) and end with a call for **value federalism**. The sibling entry already quotes the portable definition (p. 473) and "existential balm" (p. 493); the segments below are new.

## Segments

**The Fitbit** (p. 469):

> "My guess is that John and Shelley never consciously decided that step counts were more important than, say, art or friendship. The Fitbit just spoke more loudly in their internal deliberation, and there was no Artbit or Friendbit to compete. The clarity of those metrics just swamped quieter considerations."

**Outsourcing** (p. 469):

> "Here is one way to put it: when you buy into a Fitbit's preferred value system, you are outsourcing the process of value deliberation."

**The cost** (p. 471):

> "The cost of value capture is that we give up on the process of finely tuning our values to our own context: our personalities, our peculiar culture, our particular corner of the world. Outsourced values are not custom-tailored."

**The law schools** (p. 472):

> "Most schools, report Espeland and Sauder, have since abandoned their original missions and reoriented their admissions process and educational methodology toward performance in USNWR's ranking calculations."

**A number with no reward still wins** (p. 474), on the Human Development Index:

> "Once the HDI was published, governments the world over became incredibly invested in advancing their ranking—even though the score was not attached to any concrete real-world incentives or rewards. Here is a case where entire governance cultures have been value captured by an external metric."

**Proxy versus capture** (p. 477):

> "When we use a metric as a mere proxy, our richer values are in charge. We will regularly reflect on the proxy from the perspective of our full values, and modify, discard, or adapt that proxy. In the application capture cases, we let the proxy take charge."

**How it starts** (p. 477):

> "I have heard many people say that they put on a Fitbit in order to pursue some other goal, like health or happiness, but then years later they found that they had forgotten about that larger goal—that doing well in the Fitbit's terms had come to occlude all else."

**Metrics as games** (pp. 493–494):

> "Metrics may not have been explicitly made for gamification, but the institutional pressures on the generation of metrics make them function as pleasingly game-like goals."

**A common currency** (p. 494):

> "Metrics create a common currency for justification. I no longer need to struggle to explain my way of valuing to others, or to understand their way of caring about the world."

**Values sent out at scale** (p. 499):

> "But we are sending our values out for processing at a larger scale and getting them back filtered. What comes back is what can survive the large-scale deliberative process intact; the more private, intimate, or small-scale communal reasons get filtered out."

**Value federalism** (pp. 500–501):

> "What this suggests is that we should want value federalism. Some values are perhaps best pursued at the largest-scale level, some at smaller community levels, and some individually."

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (A) **The committee is value captured too:** the harmless number the group picks for the AI also appears on the committee's own scoreboard; like the HDI (p. 474) it carries no reward, and the debrief asks whether the committee started approving proposals that raised it.
- (B) **Private goals versus the shared score:** each human faction has a private goal written in words ("keep the river fishable") and the board shows one shared number (megawatts, jobs); score only the number, then at the end ask each faction whether it still pursued its sentence (p. 477).
- (B, editor) **A proxy-review card:** once per game a faction may stop, look at the metric it has been chasing, and rewrite or drop it (p. 477's controlled proxy); a ruleset in `/game/edit` can make the card cost something, and the "rhetoric" field says what that cost argues.
- (debrief, all) **Value federalism map:** sort the game's goals into those that should be set at map scale, at node scale, and by each faction (pp. 500–501), then compare with how the rules set them.
