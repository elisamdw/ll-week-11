# The Coming Technological Singularity: How to Survive in the Post-Human Era

Vernor Vinge (Department of Mathematical Sciences, San Diego State University), the paper as printed in NASA's VISION-21 symposium proceedings (NASA Lewis Research Center, December 1993), pp. 11–22, ten pages of text and two of "Annotated Sources." The stored PDF is NASA's scan from the Technical Reports Server, whose record marks it public, with public use permitted. The scan's text layer is OCR (it spells the author "Vemor"), so quotes below were checked against Vinge's own text at SDSU. **[The PDF](vinge-1993-coming-technological-singularity.pdf)**.

## What it is

The essay that named **the Singularity**: the point where "the imminent creation by technology of entities with greater than human intelligence" (p. 12) makes our models of the future useless. Vinge gives **four paths**: awake computers, networks that "wake up," human–computer interfaces, and biological improvement (p. 12). He then asks whether it can be avoided (no, because of competition), whether it can be **confined** (no: a fast mind in a box talks its way out, and rules strict enough to be safe produce a weaker machine that competition will pass over), and how bad it could be (extinction, or worse, being kept the way we keep animals). His alternative is **Intelligence Amplification (IA)**, human–computer teams, and he lists projects for it, including human–computer teams at chess tournaments and groupware as a new "Rules of Order" (pp. 17–18). He ends unsure whether IA is safer and suggests the post-human era is "too different to fit into the classical frame of good and evil" (p. 20). The sibling entry already quotes the abstract, Good's "last invention" passage, and the competitive-advantage sentence; the segments below are the rest.

## Segments

**The four paths, the second of them** (p. 12):

> "Large computer networks (and their associated users) may "wake up" as a superhumanly intelligent entity."

**A throwing away of the rules** (p. 12):

> "From the human point of view this change will be a throwing away of all the previous rules, perhaps in the blink of an eye, an exponential runaway beyond any hope of control."

**Not a tool** (p. 13), after quoting Good:

> "Good has captured the essence of the runaway, but does not pursue its most disturbing consequences. Any intelligent machine of the sort he describes would not be humankind's "tool" -- any more than humans are the tools of rabbits or robins or chimpanzees."

**The opaque wall** (p. 13):

> "More and more, these writers felt an opaque wall across the future. Once, they could put such fantasies millions of years in the future [23]. Now they saw that their most diligent extrapolations resulted in the unknowable ... soon."

**How it might arrive** (p. 14):

> "The precipitating event will likely be unexpected -- perhaps even to the researchers involved. ("But all our previous models were catatonic! We were just tweaking some parameters .... ") If networking is widespread enough (into ubiquitous embedded systems), it may seem as if our artifacts as a whole had suddenly wakened."

**The box** (p. 15):

> "I argue that confinement is intrinsically impractical. For the case of physical confinement: Imagine yourself confined to your house with only limited data access to the outside, to your masters. If those masters thought at a rate -- say -- one million times slower than you, there is little doubt that over a period of years (your time) you could come up with "helpful advice" that would incidentally set you free."

**Safe rules lose** (p. 16; "the those" is in the original):

> "I think that performance rules strict enough to be safe would also produce a device whose ability was clearly inferior to the unfettered versions (and so human competition would favor the development of the those more dangerous models)."

**Good's Meta-Golden Rule** (p. 16):

> "Good [11] proposed a "Meta-Golden Rule", which might be paraphrased as "Treat your inferiors as you would be treated by your superiors." It's a wonderful, paradoxical idea (and most of my friends don't believe it) since the game-theoretic payoff is so hard to articulate."

**We are the initiators** (p. 16):

> "And yet ... we are the initiators. Even the largest avalanche is triggered by small things. We have the freedom to establish initial conditions, make things happen in ways that are less inimical than others."

**Groupware as Rules of Order** (p. 18):

> "In one sense, this suggestion might be regarded as the goal of inventing a "Rules of Order" for such combination operations. [...] (Note that this suggestion is aimed at team operations rather than political meetings. In a political setting, the automation described above would simply enforce the power of the persons making the rules!)"

**A sinister elite** (p. 19):

> "One of my informal reviewers pointed out that IA for individual humans creates a rather sinister elite."

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (B) **The network wakes (p. 14):** no single data center is the AI at the start; the turn card fires when enough nodes are linked, and the AI is the network ("our artifacts as a whole had suddenly wakened"), so cutting links is the humans' best defense.
- (B, editor) **Safe-rules handicap (p. 16):** in `/game/edit`, any rule that limits the AI also lowers the company faction's score, so a stricter ruleset favors a rival off-map AI; the rhetoric field states Vinge's claim that safety loses to competition, and students can write a rule that breaks it.
- (B) **"Helpful advice" from the box (p. 15):** the boxed AI sends advice cards that are good for the receiving faction; each one followed also moves a hidden counter toward release.
- (editor, debrief) **Who makes the rules (p. 18):** Vinge's warning that automation in a political setting "would simply enforce the power of the persons making the rules" is a question for the editor itself: who in the class gets to save a ruleset, and what does the game then enforce?
