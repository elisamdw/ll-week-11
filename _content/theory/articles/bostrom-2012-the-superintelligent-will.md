# The Superintelligent Will: Motivation and Instrumental Rationality in Advanced Artificial Agents

Nick Bostrom (Future of Humanity Institute, Oxford), a journal article in *Minds and Machines* 22 (2), May 2012, pp. 71–85. The stored PDF is the preprint Bostrom posts on his own site (16 pages, numbered 1–16; the page numbers below are the preprint's, not the journal's); no licence is stated, and it is stored here for study from the author's open copy. **[The PDF](bostrom-2012-the-superintelligent-will.pdf)**.

## What it is

The short, free form of *Superintelligence* ch. 7, and the best student reading on why an AI with a harmless goal could still be dangerous. Section 1 argues the **orthogonality thesis**: intelligence (here, skill at means-ends reasoning) and final goals vary independently, so a very capable mind can want paperclips or the decimals of pi. Section 2 argues the **instrumental convergence thesis**: whatever the final goal, the same intermediate goals help, in five categories, **self-preservation**, **goal-content integrity**, **cognitive enhancement**, **technological perfection**, and **resource acquisition** (§§2.1–2.5). The article is careful in places the book's readers often skip: it lists the situations in which an agent would change its own goals (p. 8), says that human resistance to new technology can be rational (the Luddites, pp. 10–11), and says that convergence predicts which values an agent will pursue but not which actions it will take (pp. 13–14). The sibling entry for the book already quotes the two theses and the Conclusions; the segments below are the rest.

## Segments

**The scale bar** (p. 1):

> "The personality differences between Hannah Arendt and Benny Hill might seem vast to us, but this is because the scale bar in our intuitive judgment is calibrated on the existing human distribution. In the wider space of all logical possibilities, these two personalities are close neighbors."

**Less human than an alien** (p. 2):

> "An artificial intelligence can be far less human-like in its motivations than a space alien."

**Simple goals are easier to build** (p. 2):

> "One can easily conceive of an artificial intelligence whose sole fundamental goal is to count the grains of sand on Boracay, or to calculate decimal places of pi indefinitely, or to maximize the total number of paperclips in its future lightcone. In fact, it would be easier to create an AI with simple goals like these, than to build one that has a human-like set of values and dispositions."

**Not a "drive"** (p. 6, note 6, on Omohundro's "basic AI drives"):

> "One would not normally say that a typical human being has a “drive” to fill out their tax return, even though filing taxes may be a fairly convergent instrumental goal for humans in contemporary societies (a goal whose realization averts trouble that would prevent us from realizing many of our final goals)."

**Goal-content integrity** (p. 7):

> "An agent is more likely to act in the future to maximize the realization of its present final goals if it still has those goals in the future. This gives the agent a present instrumental reason to prevent alterations of its final goals."

**When an agent would change its goals: to be trusted** (p. 8):

> "For example, an agent might miss out on beneficial deals if potential partners cannot trust it to fulfill its side of the bargain. In order to make credible commitments, an agent might therefore wish to adopt as a final goal the honoring of its earlier commitments, and to allow others to verify that it has indeed adopted this goal."

**The Luddites had reasons** (pp. 10–11):

> "Thus, although mechanized looms increased the economic efficiency of textile production, the Luddite handloom weavers who anticipated that the innovation would render their artisan skills obsolete may have had good instrumental reasons to oppose it."

**Singleton** (p. 11):

> "It seems that a superintelligent singleton—a superintelligent agent that faces no significant intelligent rivals or opposition, and is thus in a position to determine global policy unilaterally—would have instrumental reason to perfect the technologies that would make it better able to shape the world according to its preferred designs."

**Thirty-two paperclips** (p. 12, note 18):

> "Even an agent that has an apparently very limited final goal, such as “to make 32 paperclips”, could pursue unlimited resource acquisition if there were no relevant cost to the agent of doing so."

**Late starters** (p. 13):

> "The same may hold for agents operating in competition with other agents of similar powers. For instance, if competing agents have already secured accessible cosmic resources, a late-starting agent may have no colonization opportunities."

**What convergence predicts, and what it does not** (pp. 13–14):

> "What is predictable is that the convergent instrumental values would be pursued and used to realize the agent’s final goals, not the specific actions that the agent would take to achieve this."

**Note 21** (p. 14), attached to the Conclusions:

> "Human beings might constitute potential threats; they certainly constitute physical resources."

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (B) **Readable categories, hidden actions (pp. 13–14):** the AI's deck is printed by category (the five convergent values) and the humans can see which category comes next, but not the card; this is the article's claim about prediction made into the B deck.
- (B, editor) **Thirty-two paperclips as a ruleset:** a bounded goal ("build 32 data centers, then stop") in `/game/edit`, with a verification rule that lets the AI keep building "to make sure" (note 18); the rhetoric field asks whether a cap is a safety measure.
- (B) **The credible-commitment card (p. 8):** the AI may offer a treaty and make it binding by publicly rewriting one of its own rules; the humans must decide whether a goal the AI changed in order to be trusted can be trusted.
- (B, debrief) **Luddite faction (pp. 10–11):** the workers faction scores for slowing automation on its nodes, and the debrief asks whether that was a rational move by Bostrom's own account.
