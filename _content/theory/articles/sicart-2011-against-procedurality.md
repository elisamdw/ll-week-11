## What it is

The reply to Bogost's procedural rhetoric, written from game design and critical theory. Sicart opens with Tati's *Play Time*, where straight modernist lines give way to curves, and makes that the program: play bends the rules. Section one reads the **proceduralists** (Bogost's *Unit Operations* and *Persuasive Games*, Flanagan, and designers who wrote **author statements**: Brathwaite and Sharp's "The Mechanic is the Message," Rod Humble's *The Marriage*, Jason Rohrer's *Passage*, Jonathan Blow's *Braid*). Section two adapts Horkheimer and Adorno's instrumental reason into **instrumental play**: play that serves a meaning fixed in advance by the designer, which turns play into labor. Section three names the flaw: proceduralism leaves out the player, and its favored games are single-player puzzles or resource managers with few operations, where "the designer plays the player." RS already quotes the abstract and the demand for "an orthogonal analysis of play" (`NB/_context/projects/50-in-focus/ll-weeks/fall-2026/11-ending-20261120/research/learning-games-classics.md`); the segments below are the rest.

## Segments

**The plot** (Introduction):

> "On the DVD commentary for the film, critic Philip Kemp paraphrases Tati: “If Playtime has a plot, it's how the curve comes to reassert itself over the straight line” (Tati, 1967; minute 7:20). In this article I will argue that this line should also be the plot for game studies."

**What proceduralists believe** (The Proceduralists):

> "For the proceduralists, a game means what the rules mean, and understanding what games are is to understand what their rules describe. Players are important, but only as activators of the process that sets the meanings contained in the game in motion."

**The author statement problem** (The Proceduralists), on Rohrer's *Passage*:

> "It seems that even though the idea of rules and procedurality as conveyors of meaning is present, those game designers that have embraced that approach still need to make the statements more clear, to make sure that their players “get” the games. If rules contain the meaning, what is the need for an author statement?"

**Play as labor** (Understanding Instrumental Play):

> "Proceduralism, with its call for systems at the core of the essence of games and its disregard for expressive or ineffective play, turns the act of playing a game into a labor-like action, into work towards an externally decided, predetermined, and rational outcome designed by others than the players."

**Rules frame play** (Understanding Instrumental Play):

> "Games structure play, facilitate it by means of rules. This is not to say that rules determine play: they focus it, they frame it, but they are still subject to the very act of play. Play, again, is an act of appropriation of the game by players."

**Players change the rules** (Understanding Instrumental Play):

> "However, play is not that simple to guide, not even through rules. Empirical data shows how players constantly negotiate rules, adopt new ones and discard those afforded by the designers (Taylor 2006, 2009)."

**Whose expression** (Understanding Instrumental Play):

> "In the proceduralist realm, players do not express themselves by play: they express the designers by means of their actions guided by the rules."

**The designer plays the player** (Against Procedurality):

> "But these games are seldom playful in a mechanical, procedural sense: these are single player, puzzle or resource management games, with only few “operations” available to players, and a very limited space of possibility in which players can express themselves. In fact, these games often force players to reconfigure a particular set of actions in the way a designer has thought them, explicitly abolishing many possible instances of player creation and appropriation. The designer, in this case, plays the player."

**An army of players** (Against Procedurality):

> "To write against procedurality is to sing the body, the presence, the player. Against procedurality an army of players stand and play, breaking the rules, misunderstanding the processes, appropriating the spaces of play and taking them somewhere else, where not even the designer can reach."

**The rules as a dialogue** (Against Procedurality, closing):

> "Play, however, is personal, individual, and communitarian, played with others, for others, in an intensely, deeply personal way. And politics and ethics are personal, too. Therefore, when a player engages with a game, we enter the realm of play, where the rules are a dialogue and the message, a conversation."

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (editor) **The "rhetoric" field is an author statement:** Sicart's question ("If rules contain the meaning, what is the need for an author statement?") applies to it; after a playtest, groups compare what their rhetoric field claimed with what players did, and revise one or the other.
- (B) **House rules are legal:** a table may propose a new rule mid-game by vote (Taylor's players "adopt new ones and discard those afforded by the designers"); the rule is logged, and the debrief reads the log as the play-side analysis Sicart asks for.
- (B, C) **Not a single-player puzzle:** keep the human factions' private goals and negotiation open-ended, so the game is not one of the "few operations" resource managers Sicart criticizes; the AI may play the straight line, the humans the curve.
- (debrief, all) **Bogost and Sicart as two questions:** first ask what the rules argued (Bogost), then what the players did with them that no rule predicted (Sicart).
