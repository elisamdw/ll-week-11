# The Rhetoric of Video Games

Ian Bogost (Georgia Institute of Technology), a 24-page chapter in Katie Salen's *The Ecology of Games* (MIT Press, 2008), pp. 117–140, one of the six open-access volumes of the MacArthur series on digital media and learning. The chapter's citation page carries the licence: "Copyright: © 2008 Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Published under Creative Commons Attribution-Noncommercial-No Derivative Works Unported 3.0 license." The stored file is the **whole volume** as MIT Press serves it (2.4 MB); the chapter is PDF pages 116–138. **[The PDF](bogost-2008-rhetoric-of-video-games.pdf)**.

## What it is

The short, classroom-ready version of *Persuasive Games* (2007), written for teachers and parents. Bogost starts with his five-year-old discovering that in *Animal Crossing* more possessions mean more space and more debt, then builds the argument in four steps: **play** as "the free space of movement within a more rigid structure" (Salen and Zimmerman, p. 120), **procedurality** (Murray, Crawford's **process intensity**), **rhetoric** (Aristotle to Burke to visual rhetoric), and **procedural rhetoric**. Its examples are the ones the book made famous (*The McDonald's Videogame*, *America's Army*) plus *Take Back Illinois*, a policy game Bogost designed for the Illinois House Republicans in 2004, in which four linked subgames feed each other's scores. It ends with a program for schools: teach kids to write "computer arguments" as well as computer programs. The sibling entry already quotes the procedural-rhetoric definition (p. 125), the McDonald's passage (p. 127), and *Animal Crossing* (p. 119); the segments below are the rest of the chapter.

## Segments

**Games make claims** (p. 119):

> "This is an important distinction: video games are not just stages that facilitate cultural, social, or political practices; they are also media where cultural values themselves can be represented—for critique, satire, education, or commentary. When understood in this way, we can learn to read games as deliberate expressions of particular perspectives. In other words, video games make claims about the world, which players can understand, evaluate, and deliberate."

**A house rule carries a value** (p. 121), on hide-and-seek where the older player counts longer:

> "This rule is not merely instrumental; it suggests a value of equity in the game and its players."

**Rules make the meaning** (p. 121):

> "The rules do not merely create the experience of play—they also construct the meaning of the game. That is to say, the gestures, experiences, and interactions a game's rules allow (and disallow) make up the game's significance."

**Writing procedurally** (p. 122):

> "To write procedurally, one authors code that enforces rules to generate some kind of representation, rather than authoring the representation itself."

**Arguments about failure** (p. 126):

> "Artifacts that deploy procedural rhetoric can also make arguments about how things don't work just as easily as they can make arguments about how they do."

**No game is neutral** (p. 128):

> "Like all cultural artifacts, no video game is produced in a cultural vacuum. All bear the biases of their creators."

**The risk in games that train professionals** (p. 130), on *America's Army*:

> "However, America's Army also shows that epistemic games bear a risk: sometimes, we may want to question the values of professional practices rather than assume those values blindly."

**A node goes dark** (p. 132), the medical-malpractice subgame of *Take Back Illinois*:

> "In the medical malpractice subgame, maintaining a high threshold on noneconomic damages keeps insurance rates high, which is likely to cause doctors to leave the state. Once this happens, the medical office dims and the player can no longer treat citizens there."

**Linked systems** (p. 132):

> "In public forums, policy issues are often discussed independently, even though most are bound to one another in significant ways. To communicate the rhetoric of interrelations, Take Back Illinois maintained a set of scores for each subgame and used those scores as inputs for settings in other games."

**Persuaded, not brainwashed** (p. 133):

> "In playing the game, the player is not “brainwashed” or otherwise fooled into adopting the candidates' policy position. Rather he is afforded an understanding of that position for further inquiry, agreement, or disapproval."

**Students write arguments in rules** (p. 137):

> "In addition to using video games to teach kids how to write computer programs (procedural literacy), we can use them to teach kids how to write computer arguments (procedural rhetoric). When kids program, just as when they write, they can learn to make their own claims about the world in the form of processes."

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (editor) **The "rhetoric" field is Bogost's p. 137 assignment:** each rule a group writes in `/game/edit` is a "computer argument," and the field asks the p. 121 question of it: what value does this rule carry, as the longer count in hide-and-seek carries equity?
- (B) **Nodes that dim:** borrow *Take Back Illinois*'s rule (p. 132): when a policy setting pushes a node past a threshold (water price, grid load), the node goes dark on the map and stops working for every faction, an argument about how things don't work (p. 126).
- (B) **Scores as inputs:** each faction's score in one round feeds a setting in another faction's next round (p. 132), so tight coupling is written into the rule table where students can read it.
- (debrief, A) **Whose biases?** After play, each group names one rule in the AI's deck and says whose bias it carries (p. 128), and whether the game trained them to accept a profession's values or to question them (p. 130).
