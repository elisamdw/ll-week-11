# Unit Operations: An Approach to Videogame Criticism

Ian Bogost's first book (MIT Press, 2006), a method for criticizing games, and other media, as systems built from small, discrete units of behavior.

## The argument

Bogost contrasts **unit operations**, discrete, separable bits of behavior performed by or on single entities, with **system operations**, totalizing structures that explain a whole at once (his example of system thinking is structuralism, per Emily Short's review). He draws the unit idea from **object-oriented programming**, where each object carries its own behaviors, and from **cellular automata**, where simple local rules produce complex patterns; so the book argues that "any medium—from videogames to poetry, literature, cinema, or art—can be read as a configurative system of discrete, interlocking units of meaning" (publisher's description). **Procedural criticism**, in practice, means listing what a game lets the player and the world do, then asking what those operations mean together: *Grand Theft Auto III*'s many freedoms, for instance, are all sorted by legality. Part III, "Procedural Subjectivity," holds the passage this game most needs: the **simulation gap**, the space between a simulation's rules and the player's own understanding of the system, and **simulation fever**, which Jeffries glosses as the strong reaction that gap provokes; Short adds his point that a simulation's bias lies as much in what it leaves out as in what it models. In the 2008 chapter he names *SimCity* as the standard example of a game that teaches "urban planning" through its model ([Bogost, Persuasive Games](bogost-persuasive-games.md), p. 123), and a Civilization-like game about AI infrastructure is exactly the kind of system the book asks us to read unit by unit.

## Passages

> "A simulation is the gap between the rule-based representation of a source system and a user's subjectivity."

*Unit Operations*, p. 107 (unverified: seen quoted in L. B. Jeffries's PopMatters review, 2009; not checked against the book). Jeffries also quotes, without a page, Bogost's two failed responses to that gap: "simulation resignation," which "implies the blind acceptance of the limited results of a simulation," and (in paraphrase) "simulation denial," the rejection of simulations because they simplify.

> "In GTA, every decision both includes and excludes another possibility, and thus choosing to drive an ambulance instead of bludgeoning a passerby for some cash to buy a new handgun develops fluid meanings that signify in relation to other possible unit operations."

*Unit Operations*, p. 168 (unverified: seen quoted in Emily Short's review, *electronic book review*, 2008; not checked against the book).

## Critiques and conversations

- Emily Short, "The Unit Is in the Eye of the Beholder," *electronic book review*, Oct. 2, 2008: "Any concept that can be applied to so many disciplines is either extremely powerful or too general to be of any practical use." She finds it useful anyway, especially on simulation and bias, and asks for an account of higher-level units (in *Portal*, "dispose of an intelligent being" built out of shoot, move, drop).
- The book is the ground for [Bogost, Persuasive Games](bogost-persuasive-games.md), which turns unit analysis into a theory of argument; Sicart's reply to that is in [Sicart, Play Matters](sicart-play-matters.md).
- Bogost later uses "enframing," a term he says he developed near the end of this book, against Facebook games: see [Bogost, Cow Clicker](bogost-cow-clicker.md).

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (B) Each index-card infrastructure node as a unit with its own small rule (inputs, outputs, what makes it fail), so the AI's spread and the Perrow cascade emerge from the units, not from a global script.
- (B, C) A visible "not modeled" list in `rules.json` (labor, heat, local politics, chip supply), with a debrief question: which omission changes the game's argument most?
- (A, B, C) A simulation-gap debrief: players say where the rule table disagrees with their sense of how AI infrastructure works, then argue "resignation" (trust the model) against "denial" (dismiss it).
- (C) Two levels of units shown side by side: the AI player's upgrades on the incremental screen, and the map units each upgrade produces (a new data center, a drained reservoir).
