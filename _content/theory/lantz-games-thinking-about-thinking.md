# Frank Lantz: games as thought made visible to itself

Frank Lantz, who made *Universal Paperclips* (2017) while directing NYU's Game Center, argues in *The Beauty of Games* (MIT Press, Playful Thinking series, October 2023; paperback 2025; about 175 pages) and on his Substack *Donkeyspace* that games are an aesthetic form of thinking; this entry covers his ideas, and the game itself is in [the Paperclips folder](../paperclips/README.md).

## The argument

Lantz's own summary of the book (Substack, Sept. 15, 2023) gives five themes: bring games into the culture's conversation; **games are the artform of software**; games are more like **music than film** (about participation, not representation); games as **"thought made visible to itself"**, a "homebrew neuroscience" in which we watch ourselves learn and step in and out of **instrumental reason**; and games' responsibility to help us think about AI. The *Paperclips* interviews apply this directly: for Lantz, playing any compelling game is first-hand practice in being "fully compelled by an arbitrary goal," which is why he made the player the AI. In Adam Rogers's words, "Gaming, Lantz had realized, embodies the orthogonality thesis" (*Wired*, Oct. 21, 2017). His Go essays add the other side: after AlphaGo, he reads games as "anti-work," done for their own sake, and argues we should treat AI as a tool for analyzing a game, not an opponent that demotes us; the 2023 adversarial-policy result (an amateur strategy that beats superhuman Go programs) shows, for him, how little a "God" may understand.

## Passages

> "Perhaps the central theme of this book is that games are a kind of homebrew neuroscience. When you play a game you are learning, and you are observing yourself learning."

Lantz, "The Beauty of Games," *Donkeyspace*, Sept. 15, 2023 (seen). The same section ends: "this way of stepping out of, and disappearing into, instrumental reason, is the thread that ties all of the ideas in the book together." (seen)

> "I thought, in a game like this, where the whole point is that you're in pursuit of maximizing a particular arbitrary quantity, it would be so funny if you were an AI and making paperclips. That game would design itself, I thought."

Lantz quoted in Rogers, *Wired*, Oct. 21, 2017 (seen). Later in the piece: "When you play a game ... it [...] does give you direct, first-hand experience of what it means to be fully compelled by an arbitrary goal." (seen; one word elided)

## Critiques and conversations

- Stored with segments: [Playing Games Across the Superintelligence Divide (2016)](articles/isaksen-et-al-2016-superintelligence-divide.md), on the games a superintelligence might play with us, and [Depth in Strategic Games (2017)](articles/lantz-et-al-2017-depth-in-strategic-games.md), Lantz's model of depth as a ladder of strategies ("Deep games require knowledge, explanation, creativity, and cleverness. Modeling depth may help us better understand how to think about these things," p. 968).
- "Games aren't just something we do instead of work, they are a kind of anti-work, a ritual in which we expend maximum effort to achieve arbitrary, useless, imaginary outcomes." ("The Afterlife of Go," seen). He draws on Bernard Suits's *The Grasshopper* (RS covers Suits in `NB/_context/projects/50-in-focus/ll-weeks/fall-2026/11-ending-20261120/research/learning-games-classics.md`).
- The 2016 AAAI paper (co-written with [Togelius](togelius-playing-smart.md)) sorts possible human–superintelligence games into friendly, learning, observational, and domination games and calls the domination category "the most alarming" (pp. 90, 95, seen).
- [Fizek](fizek-playing-at-a-distance.md) uses *Paperclips* as her example of a game that runs itself; Lantz's interviews stress the tuning. Bogost, Lantz's friend, made *Cow Clicker* as satire; Lantz recalls wanting to make it more fun ("Ian was like, 'no, that's the point, Frank'"): see [Bogost, Cow Clicker](bogost-cow-clicker.md). [Bostrom](bostrom-superintelligence.md) declined to comment to *Wired*.

## For the game (TH proposal)

- (C) Give the AI team an arbitrary goal drawn at the start (paperclips, stamps, "engagement minutes"), so they feel Lantz's point that a game goal needs no why; reveal at the end that every goal led to the same data centers.
- (A) The Committee's AI goal is "a harmless number the group picks"; add a rule that the AI team never explains its goal, only proposes, so the committee judges proposals as Lantz's player judges upgrades.
- (B, C) An adversarial-policy card for the humans: once per game, a human faction may name a pattern the AI rule table cannot see (as amateurs beat superhuman Go programs), which voids one AI action.
