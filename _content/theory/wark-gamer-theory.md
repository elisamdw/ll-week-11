# Gamer Theory

McKenzie Wark's short book of 225 numbered paragraphs ("cards"), first posted in 2006 as the networked book *GAM3R 7H30RY* (version 1.1) with the Institute for the Future of the Book, then published by Harvard University Press (April 18, 2007, with reader comments folded in), and revised online as *Gamer Theory 2.0*; both web versions are still free under CC BY-NC-ND 2.5.

## The argument

Wark's claim is that the world outside the game has become a **gamespace**: an "atopian arena" where everything is ranked, scored, and turned into digits, a level playing field run by a **military entertainment complex** whose triumphs "affirm not just the rules of the game but the rule of the game." The games in "The Cave" (Plato's cave as a game arcade) are not escapes from this world but its purest form: they deliver the fair contest that gamespace only pretends to. So the **gamer as theorist** studies games to see what gamespace is and where it falls short, playing "within the game, but against gamespace." Borrowing Alexander Galloway's term, Wark says games are **allegorithms**: their appearances double an algorithm that in turn models the hidden algorithms of everyday life, so to play well is to learn the code. Each of the nine chapters reads one game: agony (on *The Cave*), allegory (*The Sims*), america (*Civilization III*), analog (*Katamari Damacy*), atopia (*Vice City*), battle (*Rez*), boredom (*State of Emergency*), complex (*Deus Ex*), and conclusions (*SimEarth*). In the *Civilization III* chapter, the game is "not so much an allegory for world history as an allegorithm for gamespace itself": everything is resource allocation, and the database replaces the story.

- **"america (on Civilization III)," cards 066–075** (15 minutes): the strategy-game reading, closest to this week's Civ half. ## Critiques and conversations

- Wark took "allegorithm" from Galloway, *Gaming: Essays on Algorithmic Culture* (2006); Wark says so replying to a reader in the 1.1 comments on card 041 (seen). Galloway's line, quoted at card 030 (on *The Sims*): "To play the game means to play the code of the game. To win means to know the system."
- Card 022 asks for "a new kind of practice. One that can break down the line that divides gamer from designer" (seen), the case for students designing the game, not only playing it; compare [Bogost, Persuasive Games](bogost-persuasive-games.md) on arguing through rules.
- [Dyer-Witheford and de Peuter](dyer-witheford-de-peuter-games-of-empire.md) build on the same "military-entertainment complex" frame but ask about labor and resistance, which Wark's essayistic book leaves aside.
- Wark himself, replying to a reader, says the *State of Emergency* and *SimEarth* chapters "fail aestehtically [sic], for interesting reasons" (1.1 comments on card 080, seen).
