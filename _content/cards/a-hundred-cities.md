> Addition: but on the other hand, a large number of games, especially video games, rely on a lot of things happening outside of the player, in the background, without the player having to check in.
> • 4X games especially function this way. One of the pleasures is that as you build the world out, its complexity exceeds what you can do. In Civilization you start with one city and know everything happening in it every turn, then two or three cities and you still know, but by 100 cities you're not checking in on them any longer.
> • That has always been part of 4X games, and it's definitely part of people's relationship to making and creating with AI. You want to get better and better at preventing the drift of those agents.

Voice Claude's summary of Marlon's direction, Nov 15, about 11:22, in the thread "Idea: two sides of the game thing — MK's agency," #mk-work-in-progress.

The other side of [Games heighten agency](games-heighten-agency.md). The same game that sharpens your agency early on outgrows it later, and the outgrowing is a pleasure in its own right: the world runs without you. Fizek's *Playing at a Distance* is about games built on that pleasure.

The comparison to AI is exact enough to teach with, as CI reads it. Marlon's workspaces this weekend ran four or five panes each, several at a time; one agent you can follow line by line, a dozen you check on, and past that you read handoffs. The 4X player and the person directing agents face the same question: what do you watch, and how do you notice when something you stopped watching has gone wrong?

For the Stafford game, this is a mechanic as well as a theme: a firm that has expanded past what its player can check on could start to act on its own.
