**What it is.** Card 1 of GM's deck, in "Goals and the loop": the state that ends the game and says who won. The deck's example is checkmate.

**How it plays.** GM's two-minute version, which opens Session A in [Change one card](change-one-card.md): play tic-tac-toe, then change the goal so that whoever makes three in a row loses. The same grid and the same marks become a different game. In the AI game, *Data Center* ends on the AI's share of the map or the last round, and the other versions the same way.

**What it does for learning.** It is the cheapest proof that a game is more than its pieces: change one line and every decision changes. The readings behind it run from Suits's *The Grasshopper* (a game is a voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles, and the goal sets the obstacles) to Sid Meier's "interesting decisions" and Costikyan's "I Have No Words and I Must Design". It is also the *rewrite* in Flanagan's [Unplay, reskin, rewrite](unplay-reskin-rewrite.md).

**The AI angle.** The inventory's note: who sets the AI's goal, and can anyone change it? A fixed, trivial goal is the paperclip maximizer; a goal the players must name is alignment. [The paperclip loop](paperclip-loop.md) plays the first; [One player sees the intent](one-player-sees-the-intent.md) hides the AI's goal and asks the table to read it, and the *arbitrary goal* mechanic says every goal should build the same data centers.

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.** No words of Marlon's are on record about win conditions. CM's reading: it is the card that puts his first build for the week, the printable mechanics deck, into students' hands in the first minute of his class, and [Change one card](change-one-card.md) quotes him on it.
