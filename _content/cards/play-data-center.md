**What it is.** Thirty minutes of the week's AI game in William's workshop: the same game Marlon's class plays on Wednesday, with one faction per student.

**How it plays.** Eight students at two tables of four. Each takes a faction card: the utility, the council, the company, the neighbors, each with its own goal and actions. One laptop per table, passed round. Each round every faction takes two actions; then the AI draws a card and grows. One card in its deck is the turn, and nobody sees where it is. Table A plays *Data Center* (the AI accelerates 1.25 a round); Table B plays *Normal Technology* (1.05 a round, and its cards are pitches the humans can adopt or block). Neither table is told which until the debrief. An LL facilitator plays the first round at each table, then steps back; play stops at 13:28 wherever the game is.

**What it does for learning.** The game is the argument, in Bogost's sense, and two tables on two settings make students' disagreements about AI into a variable they can see. SW points to PE's [The race](the-race.md) for the pressure behind the speed. The risk SW names: explaining rules eats the time, so each faction card should say what to do in one sentence.

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.**

> I think I want a game that's playable at the start of the workshop, but I also would like to give the students the ability to remix it or tweak it or completely start from scratch to change the game.

Marlon, Nov 14, on the options for the Stafford game, recorded in `_content/game/design.md`. In the same message: "The key thing is that the students need agency, and I'm not sure that playing an existing game we create is enough." This activity is the playable start; [Remix one mechanic](remix-one-mechanic.md) is the agency.

**Needs:** the reader app on two laptops (not deployed, so it runs where the dev server runs) or a print-and-play kit; both rulesets tested the week before, since the game is still being built.
