**What it is.** Card 30 of GM's deck, in "Resources and economy": hold more of the board than the others. The deck's examples are Go and *Risk*; it also came through the ENGL 189VG workshop stations (October 22).

**How it plays.** GM's two-minute version: take turns placing sticky notes on a grid, and hold a square when you surround it. In the AI game the AI spreads across linked nodes, and *Data Center* ends if it holds half the map.

**Where it is in the week.** Risk is one of the three boxes in [Take apart a board game](take-apart-a-board-game.md), and [Photo to game state](photo-to-game-state.md) turns a board's territory into pins. [The room is the board](the-room-is-the-board.md) plays it with bodies: one person holds a place, two take it back. In William's workshop, [Cards become the map](cards-become-the-map.md) makes the students' own index cards the territory.

**What it does for learning.** The inventory's note on AI: the AI's territory is infrastructure, and control of the whole map is Bostrom's singleton. Its readings pull the other way too. Easterling's *Extrastatecraft* treats infrastructure as a form of power outside law; Mukherjee's *Playing Subaltern* and Pobłocki's "Becoming-state" ask what territorial strategy games teach about empire. A class can see that "hold the board" is a claim about the world, not only a scoring rule.
