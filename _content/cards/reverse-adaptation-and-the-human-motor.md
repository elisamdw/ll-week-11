**The readings.** For the workshop day William assigns Langdon Winner's "Artifice and Order" (from *Autonomous Technology*, 1977) and Anson Rabinbach's "From Mimetic Machines to Digital Organisms" (2018). Winner's claim, in TH's summary from secondary sources: not that machines have wills, but that large technical systems set their own requirements and people adjust their ends to fit the means, which he calls "reverse adaptation." Rabinbach's earlier *The Human Motor* treated the working body as an engine with fatigue as its limit; this chapter carries the figure forward to automata and digital organisms (from the publisher's description). SW notes neither text has been opened by the LL.

**What they give the week's games.** Two mechanics that make the readings playable rather than illustrated (SW's proposal, after TH). Reverse adaptation: when the AI builds in a faction's region, that faction rewrites one of its goals to suit the new infrastructure, and nobody forces it; at the end, set each faction's first and last goals side by side. Fatigue: human factions have an energy track that actions drain, while AI nodes never tire, and labor can strike. Fizek's idle games already put the player in Winner's position: you set the system going, then adjust yourself to what it needs.

**Where it is played.** Step 2 of the debrief, [What the mechanic argues](what-the-mechanic-argues.md), is the reverse-adaptation check, on the day Winner's chapter is assigned. The homework, [Play Paperclips and log the easy moments](play-paperclips-and-log-the-easy-moments.md), asks students to log the moments the game decided for them. Fatigue and the strike are in [A logistics and labor faction](a-logistics-and-labor-faction.md).

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.**

> It's where we'll reflect thematically on ideas of AI [running?] on its own, potentially beyond human control. That'll be the theme of the game.
