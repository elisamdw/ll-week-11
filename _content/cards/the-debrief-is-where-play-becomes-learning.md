In a randomized trial, residents who ran a simulated crisis without a debrief did not improve, and those debriefed did; meta-analyses of debriefs put the gain at d = 0.67 to 0.79. For a game about a system, a debrief can ask four questions about what the rules argue, put the blame on the structure rather than the players, and end with the rule each team would change, which leads straight into the remix.

**The answer to** [How do you debrief a game so it teaches?](how-do-you-debrief-a-game.md), from a research pass on Nov 16; the full note is `W11/research/debriefing-games.md`.

**What the field says.** The simulation-and-gaming literature treats the debrief as the phase where play turns into learning, and says its shape should follow what the game is for (Peters and Vissers 2004; Crookall 2010). A game with open objectives, which students will change, asks the most of a debrief.

**The evidence.** Savoldelli et al. (2006): 42 anesthesia residents "randomly assigned to receive no debriefing, oral feedback, or videotape-assisted oral feedback"; only the debriefed improved: "Exposure to a simulated crisis without constructive debriefing by instructors offers little benefit to trainees." Across fields, debriefs "improve effectiveness over a control group by approximately 25% (d = .67)" (Tannenbaum and Cerasoli 2013, 46 samples); after-action reviews show "an overall d of 0.79" (Keiser and Arthur 2021, 61 studies). For games specifically the evidence is thinner: in a 2024 trial with 51 people, an in-person debrief added to a serious game improved scores on the second play but not on a knowledge test (Lee et al.).

**Formats that fit fifteen minutes.** PEARLS (Eppich and Cheng 2015): reactions, what happened, analysis, take-aways, with Plus/Delta as its quick version. Liberating Structures' What? So What? Now What? The after-action review's questions: what we set out to do, what happened, why, what next.

**For games about systems and arguments.** A 2012 UT Austin lesson plan on procedural rhetoric asks: "What are the rules of the system?"; "What is the significance of these rules (over other rules)?"; "What claims about the world do these rules make?"; "How do I respond to those claims?" In John Sterman's Beer Game debrief at MIT, when a student blames a teammate, he points to the game's structure instead (*MIT Technology Review*, 2013). Forester, Jett and Holtey (2024) replaced the post-game discussion with redesign, asking "What would you change in the simulation to create different results or outcomes?"; students defended their changes with course concepts, though the revisions were not played.

**For Wednesday and Nov 23, as KR reads it.** The researcher's fifteen-minute run (its own synthesis, not a source's): two minutes of one-word reactions, three of what each team tried, six on the four rule questions, four on one rule each team would change. On Wednesday, the remix that follows can be the play of that change, which goes one step past the 2024 paper; on Nov 23, [What the mechanic argues](what-the-mechanic-argues.md) is the four questions' natural home.
