**What it is.** Twenty-five minutes at tables with the LL's Monopoly, Risk and Life. Students are the computer: they enforce the rules and keep the state by hand.

**How it plays.** Before the first roll, the overhead camera (or a phone held high) photographs the set-up board; the next activity needs it. Each table plays for ten minutes. Then, on a large sheet, four columns:

- **Rules:** what is allowed and how it ends. Who enforced each one at your table?
- **Mechanics:** the verbs, what players did, matched to cards in the deck. A verb the deck lacks gets a new card.
- **Game state:** what is true right now that the game needs to remember.
- **Other game elements:** the rest of Schell's tetrad: story, aesthetics (the board, the money), technology (dice, cardboard).

Each table reports the one mechanic it would remove first, and what that would do.

**What it does for learning.** It makes Marlon's point about rules with the students' own hands. TD expects the table that gets furthest in the rules column to find that half of what it wrote are house rules. Ten minutes of Monopoly barely leaves the start, which is fine here; one table plays faster so its board changes before the photo. The readings behind it are in TH's handout: Schell's tetrad, MDA, Sicart's "Defining Game Mechanics".

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.**

> the design of the game is much, much more than the rules

Marlon, Nov 14, in a voice chat with Claude, as transcribed in the handoff back to Claude Code (section 1). He went on to say that rules may belong more to pre-digital games, where people perform the mechanics and police the turns, while in a video game the rules don't present themselves as rules: "That's just the way the game works." In the same chat he named Monopoly, Risk and Life as the LL's games to photograph (section 4).
