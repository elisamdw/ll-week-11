Katie Salen and Eric Zimmerman, *Rules of Play* (2003), the design textbook: a game is "a system in which players engage in an artificial conflict, defined by rules, that results in a quantifiable outcome." They made Huizinga's passing "magic circle" the boundary a game draws when it begins, inside which a plastic token becomes you. The book was not opened; its lines come through Jesper Juul and a wiki, the longer magic-circle passages from their own 2003 paper drawn from the book.

---

> A game is a system in which players engage in an artificial conflict, defined by rules, that results in a quantifiable outcome.

Salen and Zimmerman, *Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals* (MIT Press, 2003), p. 96, as quoted in Jesper Juul, "The Game, the Player, the World" (2003).

> In a very basic sense, the magic circle of a game is where the game takes place. To play a game means entering into a magic circle, or perhaps creating one as a game begins. The magic circle of a game might have a physical component, like the board of a board game or the playing field of an athletic contest. But many games have no physical boundaries – arm wrestling, for example, does not require much in the way of special spaces or material. The game simply begins when one or more players decide to play

*Rules of Play*, pp. 95–96, as quoted in Jesper Juul, "The Magic Circle and the Puzzle Piece" (2008), PDF pp. 57–58.

> Within the magic circle, special meanings accrue and cluster around objects and behaviors. In effect, a new reality is created, defined by the rules of the game and inhabited by its players. Before a game of Chutes and Ladders starts, it’s just a board, some plastic pieces, and a die. But once the game begins, everything changes. Suddenly, the materials represent something quite specific. This plastic token is you. These rules tell you how to roll the die and move. Suddenly, it matters very much which plastic token reaches the end first.
>
> [...]
>
> The magic circle can define a powerful space, investing its authority in the actions of players and creating new and complex meanings that are only possible in the space of play. But it is also remarkably fragile as well, requiring constant maintenance to keep it intact.

Salen and Zimmerman, "This Is Not a Game: Play in Cultural Environments," *Level Up* (DiGRA, 2003), p. 16, a paper made of selections from the book.

> Meaningful play in a game emerges from the relationship between player action and system outcome; it is the process by which a player takes action within the designed system of a game and the system responds to the action.

*Rules of Play*, p. 34, as quoted on the University of Konstanz GameLabWiki ("Games and Rules"); only a snippet was available.

**What this gives the week, as KR reads it.** Their definition is a checklist for any game the LLUFs build: what is the system, what is the conflict, what are the rules, and what number or state ends it. The magic circle names what a session has to open, keep up and close.
