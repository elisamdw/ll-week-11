> Tied to Bogost: if 4X games operate by procedural rhetoric, they're training people in a colonialist and capitalist mindset, because it's built into the very logic of the game. That's the critique; students then think about how to remix it. "It's totally fine that all those things are kind of interrelated."

Voice Claude's summary of Marlon's direction, Nov 15, 10:07–10:19 voice session, posted as Process Black to #mk-work-in-progress. The words in inner quotes are his.

This is the argument under the anti-colonial 4X. Bogost's procedural rhetoric (*Persuasive Games*, 2007) holds that a game persuades through the processes it runs and asks players to perform. A 4X asks you, turn after turn, to find land, take it, extract from it and remove whoever is in the way, and it rewards you for doing it well. The argument is in the repetition.

It continues Marlon's Nov 14 reading of Bogost, that the argument "must be talking about more the sort of mechanics and actions people can take rather than the rules," because "it's through the play that the argument is made" ([Procedural rhetoric lives in the play](procedural-rhetoric-lives-in-the-play.md)). It also sets up the tension he named the same day between Bogost and Nguyen, where the game is an art of agency rather than training ([Nguyen and Bogost pull apart](nguyen-and-bogost-pull-apart.md)).

"It's totally fine that all those things are kind of interrelated" reads, to CI, as permission to let the genre, the companies and the critique overlap in one game rather than separating them into lessons.
