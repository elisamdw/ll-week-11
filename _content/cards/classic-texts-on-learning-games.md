> And then we should do a deep dive in the research on learning games and the gamification of learning, which is not quite as in vogue as it once was, but we should go back and check out the classic texts.

Marlon, Nov 13, dictated (`W11/marlon/20261113-games-and-interaction.md`).

"Not quite as in vogue as it once was" places the field in time. Gamification peaked as a buzzword around 2010–2013 (points, badges, leaderboards) and drew sharp critique (Bogost's "Gamification is Bullshit," 2011). Going back to the classics is a way past the buzzword to the older and better arguments.

RS gathered them on Nov 13 (`W11/research/learning-games-classics.md`), in five groups: play theory (Huizinga, Caillois, Suits), motivation (Malone, Csikszentmihalyi), games and learning (Abt, Papert, Gee, Prensky, Squire), game design (Salen and Zimmerman, Flanagan, Bogost, Sicart), and gamification and its critics (Deterding, McGonigal, Bogost). Five to start with: Huizinga's first chapter, Malone 1981, Gee 2005, Bogost 2011, and a meta-analysis (Wouters 2013 or Sailer and Homner 2020), which RS summarizes as modest gains, mostly when the game is wrapped in instruction, debriefing and group work.

That finding fits the week's design, as CI reads it: play first, then take it apart and remake it.
