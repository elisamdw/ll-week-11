**What it is.** Row 71 of the inventory, in "Time" (TH, from Sonia Fizek's work on idle games and delegated play). Each upgrade removes a click and a decision, until the player can only watch and set priorities. The examples are *Cookie Clicker* and *Universal Paperclips*.

**How it plays.** The two-minute version: each round, hand one of your decisions to a fixed rule written on a card, and play until you have none left. The proposed build for the AI game: upgrades that replace a faction's action with an automatic effect each round. Not in the game yet.

**Where it is in the week.** In [The paperclip loop](paperclip-loop.md) it meets incremental growth at the offer to let the machines build machines; after that your clicks are a vanishing share of the total. The Stafford homework, [Play Paperclips and log the easy moments](play-paperclips-and-log-the-easy-moments.md), asks students to log each moment a choice became automatic and ask who is playing at that point. SW's [A logistics and labor faction](a-logistics-and-labor-faction.md) turns it on workers: the AI's automate move can replace them.

**What it does for learning.** The inventory's note on AI: delegating to the machine one decision at a time, where the end state is watching. Its readings carry the workshop day's argument: Winner's *Autonomous Technology* and Rabinbach's human motor, which SW reads as reverse adaptation, people adjusting their ends to what the system needs. A player who notices the moment they stopped deciding has seen that argument in their own play.

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.** No words of Marlon's are on record about automation as a mechanic. CM's reading: it is the move that makes *Paperclips* what it is, in the game he asked for on November 13, and it gives William's course a mechanic for its own readings.
