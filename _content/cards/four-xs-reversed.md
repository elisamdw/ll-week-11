The games that push against 4X each change one X: *Terra Nil* restores the land and leaves "no trace," *Before We Leave* drops extermination, *Spirit Island* makes the players the land repelling the colonizers, *When Rivers Were Trails* plays a displaced Anishinaabe traveler. Critics put the problem in the rules: the win condition, one track of progress for everyone, peoples built by switching traits off.

---

**The answer to** [What would a 4X without conquest look like?](what-would-a-4x-without-conquest-look-like.md), from a research pass on Nov 16; the full note is `W11/research/4x-without-conquest.md`.

**Games that change an X.**

- *Terra Nil* (2023): "A reverse city builder"; you restore an ecosystem, then recycle what you built and "leave no trace that you were there" (Steam).
- *Before We Leave* (2021): "no battles with neighbours for control of resources"; FingerGuns called it "more of a 3X game than a 4X."
- *Spirit Island* (2017): players are spirits of an island driving off Invaders. Its designer, R. Eric Reuss, began from another game's colonization mechanic: "this game has entirely abstracted away the people who already lived there." Critics credit it and note its limits: it protects the land more than its people, the Dahan, and gives the colonizers a history the colonized lack (Strange Matters).
- *When Rivers Were Trails* (2019, Elizabeth LaPensée with the Indian Land Tenure Foundation): pitched as "Oregon Trail meets Where the Water Tastes Like Wine," its Anishinaabeg protagonist is "displaced from their traditional territory in Minnesota" in the 1890s.
- *Syphilisation* (Nikhil Murthy, in development): a postcolonial 4X where "The real world isn't zero-sum."

**The counter-example.** Trevor Owens (2010) and Owens and Rebecca Mir (2012) on *Sid Meier's Colonization*: in its code, Native peoples are a base civilization with traits switched off, and its win condition requires trading with a European king; making them playable would take a new model, not a setting.

**What the critics say must change.** Dom Ford (*Game Studies*, 2016): *Civilization V* "co-opts non-colonial societies into imperialism, while in the process silencing their histories." Murthy (2021) calls 4X empire management "less a stewardship and more an unchecked hurtling towards greater and greater consumption," and goes after the win condition. Nick Pullen (2018) says the series "gamifies and glorifies colonialism, making it aggressively fun," and asks for power through tribute and alliance rather than borders, and for Indigenous designers.

**The levers, as the researcher sums them up from the sources:** change what winning means (several winners, no clean ending); make expansion costly or reversible; give non-player peoples their own systems; show the costs conquest games hide; involve the people represented.

**For Nov 23, as KR reads it.** The AI game already has [the firms as the players](the-firms-are-the-players.md) and [every resource names a place](every-resource-names-a-place.md); the cheapest lever is the win condition, and the sharpest question for William's students is whose places the map is made of.
