---

**What it is.** The ten-minute opener of William's workshop, on paper, right after William's own introduction. It makes the previous week's readings, Perrow and Fisch, into something students do with their hands.

**How it plays.** Each table writes twelve parts of an infrastructure on index cards (a substation, a cooling loop, a fiber line, a shift supervisor) and lays them out. They join cards that depend on each other: red tape for tight links (a failure passes at once), blue for loose (it passes next round, and someone can step in). Six tokens to spend before the failure: each turns one red link blue or hardens one card. Roll to pick a card; it fails. Flip every card a red link reaches, then every card those reach: that is the round. One card of each player's choosing can be saved; then the blue links pass the failure on. Count what fell, swap tables, and play the other table's network. A table that wants the screen plays [Cascade](cascade.md), the same rule on a laptop.

**What it does for learning.** Perrow's interaction of complexity and coupling, felt in ten minutes: the tight links decide the outcome before anyone acts. Fisch's slack is the blue tape. The tokens make loosening a choice with a cost, which SW calls the argument of the activity. The question to end on: which link would you loosen first, and what would it cost? SW's warning: students may re-explain Perrow instead of playing, so the roll starts within two minutes, and William, whose discussion of Perrow is the week before, draws the connections.

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.** No words of Marlon's are on record about this activity. The Nov 3 draft proposal to William, written for Marlon from his outline, names "Failures cascading through a tightly coupled system" as one of the games students could design. CM's reading: it also serves his point that the workshop shouldn't feel like the LL teaching in William's place, since it starts from William's readings.
