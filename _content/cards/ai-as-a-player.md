---

**The readings.** HI's first strand of the history of AI and games, "the proving ground": chess, checkers, Go and video games as the benchmarks AI was measured by. McCarthy called chess AI's "Drosophila" (in "AI as Sport", 1997); Togelius's *Playing Smart* (2018) is the book on games as both test beds for AI and things AI can play. Two moments TD picks for ten minutes of class: Deep Blue against Kasparov, and AlphaGo against Lee Sedol, where DeepMind said Move 37 in game 2 "had a 1 in 10,000 chance of being used," and Lee Sedol's Move 78 in game 4, the one human win, had the same odds (HI's item). And the turn students like: Wang et al. (2022) trained an adversary that beats superhuman Go programs with a strategy an amateur can see through. A machine can be superhuman and still have a blind spot.

**What they give the week's games.** TD's three ways a machine sits at a table: as an opponent (Deep Blue, AlphaGo), as an advisor whispering a move, and as a faction whose rules are written down, which is the AI in the week's own game, a deck of cards and not a model. The inventory's *Move 78* mechanic turns the blind spot into a rule.

**Where it is played.** [Claude takes a seat](claude-takes-a-seat.md), Session A's agreed swap, gives Claude each of the three roles and asks whether anyone tried to trick it. [The phone plays the AI](the-phone-plays-the-ai.md) is the written-rule opponent, [The committee](the-committee.md) the advisor.

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.** No words of Marlon's are on record about this history this week. CM's reading: it is background for the AI game's central question, who the AI is at the table, and it gives the Session A arc ([Session A](session-a-play-take-apart-remix.md)) a history students can place their own play in. TD's point for this class: the term has used AI as a tool and a collaborator, and here it is a player.
