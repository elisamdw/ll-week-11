**What it is.** The last twenty minutes of Session A, solo. It brings the afternoon of games back to the students' own work, two weeks before the December 2 work-in-progress showing and its installation planning.

**How it runs.** Each student picks from the deck the one mechanic that would let someone into their project: a visitor's vote, a zone they step into, a choice that branches, a spect-actor who steps in, a thing that runs while they're away. On an index card: who meets the work, what they do (the verb), what the system does back, and what it remembers (the state). They talk a neighbour through it as if the neighbour were the visitor, and the neighbour says where they got lost. Then sixty seconds at the camera, unscripted: "In my project, a visitor can … and the system …"

A project with no audience moment yet gets its first one here. A project that isn't interactive by design, such as a podcast series, picks a mechanic for how listeners find or follow it.

**What it does for learning.** The day's vocabulary (verb, state, view) becomes a design question about work students care about: how and where will someone meet your system? The sixty seconds is the course's third skill, speaking about the work on the spot.

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.**

> Interaction can be part of both games and installations.

Marlon, Nov 2, on the Story of Week 11 canvas (mirrored in `W11/story.md`). The syllabus he wrote for November 18 says the same from the course's side: "Games, interactive systems, and multiplayer AI: audiences and participants in the loop; games as systems with many, many parts. … This is the week your project meets other people."

**Privacy:** the index cards stay with the students; no names or projects go in the repo. TD proposes that Marlon model it first with an LL project.
