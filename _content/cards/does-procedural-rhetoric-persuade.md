The week turns on Nguyen against Bogost: games as the art of agency, or games as arguments made in rules. Bogost's claim is a theory. Has anyone tested whether persuasive games change what players believe or do, and what did they find?

---

**Why the week needs it.** [Play two theories of AI](play-two-theories-of-ai.md) on Wednesday and [What the mechanic argues](what-the-mechanic-argues.md) on Nov 23 both ask students what a game's rules argue. If the studies show modest or odd effects, that is a fact to bring to the debrief rather than a claim to take on faith.

**What would answer it.** Three or four empirical studies of persuasive or newsgames (attitude change, empathy, behavior), with what was measured and found; whether the effect was the rules or the story; one review or meta-analysis if there is one.

**Where to look.** Studies of *Darfur is Dying* and similar games (Peng, Lee and Heeter 2010), *Spent*, Ruggiero's work on games and empathy, *Games and Culture* and *Simulation & Gaming* on persuasive games, Google Scholar citations of Bogost 2007.
