**What it is.** Card 14 of GM's deck, in the family "Chance and information": some players know what others do not. The deck's examples are poker and *Stratego*. The inventory's note on AI: the AI knows its plan, the humans see only its moves, and the treacherous turn is a hidden-information mechanic.

**How it plays.** GM's two-minute version: tell the room a secret your scene partner must never learn. The build: each player's phone shows a private card while the projector shows only the public state.

**Where it is in the week.** No mechanic appears on more of the week's prototypes and activities. Phones carry it in [Private goals on your phone](private-goals-on-your-phone.md), [Place to commit](place-to-commit.md), [The readout](the-readout.md) and [One player sees the intent](one-player-sees-the-intent.md). [The audience layer](the-audience-layer.md) turns it round, with the watchers knowing more than the players. [Leaks](leaks.md) hides costs from the company's dashboard. In [Photo to game state](photo-to-game-state.md) it is what a photo can't see: hands, the order of the deck, whose turn it is, deals made out loud. In the AI game, it is the turn card hidden in the AI's deck.

**What it does for learning.** It separates the game state from any one player's view of it, which is the week's distinction between a game's data and what the screen shows. Costikyan's *Uncertainty in Games* sorts it from randomness as a source of uncertainty; several prototypes stack the two.

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.**

> let's just go with lipstick cams

Marlon, Nov 14, in a voice chat with Claude, as transcribed in the handoff back to Claude Code (section 5), on cameras for a Texas Hold'em table. Broadcast poker's answer to hidden information was glass-topped tables, then rail "lipstick" cameras, then RFID cards (voice Claude's history in the same section, flagged there as from memory). He also set its limit for NFC: not 52-card decks.
