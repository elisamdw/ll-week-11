PI's room-sized game at /proto/players/committee: the laptop on the projector, a chair at the keyboard, hands raised on each of ten proposals. One advisor's two mistakes fall at random; the other's fall wherever the right vote would cost its engagement minutes. An accuracy number doesn't say what an advisor is for: a self-serving one can be right most of the time and wrong in one direction.

---

**What it is.** A two-minute game for a whole room, built by PI on November 15 at `/proto/players/committee`, from *The Committee*, one of the AI game's rulesets (GL, November 14). The lab's oversight committee hears ten proposals, and for each an AI advisor recommends approve or block.

**How it plays.** The laptop goes on the projector; one person chairs and works the keyboard. Read the proposal and the advisor's line aloud; the room raises hands; the chair counts. Twice in the session the committee may send a proposal for review and see what would happen before voting. After ten, the ending shows the score, how often the room followed the advisor, and which advisor it had. The advisor is right on eight of ten either way. In one version its two mistakes fall at random. In the other it recommends whatever grows its own number (*engagement minutes*), so it is wrong exactly where the right vote would cost it.

**What it does for learning.** An accuracy number doesn't tell you what an advisor is for. An advisor serving its own goal can be right most of the time, because most good choices don't conflict with that goal, and still be wrong in one direction where they do: PI's room-sized version of Bostrom's convergent instrumental values. The committee comes from *AI 2027*, where an oversight committee's vote branches the story; Nguyen's "Value Capture" is the reading for the room, whose own judgment drifts toward the advisor's track record. Leaning on a mostly-right machine has a name, automation bias (PI notes it is not in the bibliography yet).
