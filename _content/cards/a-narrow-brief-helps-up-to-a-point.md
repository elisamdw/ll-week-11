A review of 145 studies proposes an inverted U: with too few constraints people take the obvious idea, with too many they lose heart. For newcomers a small kit lightens the load of unfamiliar options; for experienced people the same limit gets in the way. "Fewer choices is better" is weaker than its fame: a meta-analysis of 50 experiments found a mean effect near zero.

---

**The answer to** [What does a topic and a set of tools give a learner?](what-does-a-topic-and-a-set-of-tools-give-a-learner.md), from a research pass on Nov 16; the full note is `W11/research/constraints-and-learning.md`.

**The inverted U.** Acar, Tarakci and van Knippenberg (*Journal of Management*, 2019), an integrative review of 145 studies: constraints raise creativity up to a point and then lower it, and the curve "shifts leftward for radical innovation projects," so constraints start to hurt sooner when the aim is a new idea. In Haught-Tromp's rhyme experiments, creativity stayed higher after the constraint was lifted; she warns that too many make a task "deterministic."

**Newcomers and experts.** Cognitive load theory (Sweller): unfamiliar options use up working memory. Carroll and Carrithers (1984) switched off a word processor's advanced functions, a "training wheels" system, and beginners learned faster. The expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga, read through a summary): support that helps novices becomes redundant or harmful as they gain skill. So the right size of kit depends on who is in the room.

**Choice overload, with its caveat.** Iyengar and Lepper's jam study (30% bought from a small display, 3% from a large one, as reported in *Psychology Today*) sits beside a meta-analysis of 50 experiments whose mean effect was "virtually zero" (Scheibehenne et al. 2010). Chernev et al. (2015) find overload mainly when choices are complex, the task hard and preferences unclear, which describes a first-time workshop participant.

**From practice.** The Global Game Jam sets one theme and lets teams add optional "diversifiers": a fixed topic for everyone, extra limits for those who want them. Scratch's "low floor, high ceiling, wide walls," and Dasgupta and Hill's evidence that widening the walls, by giving Scratch users cloud variables, increased engagement and learning. Against the grain: a design-education case (Thoring and Mueller 2012) found more creativity with fewer constraints. The classroom evidence for constrained kits is mostly small and qualitative.

**For the LL's workshops, as KR reads it.** One topic and a small kit for the room, with the Global Game Jam's move for mixed rooms: optional extra tools or limits for people who already know the basics. For a brief whose aim is a new idea, keep the limits few.
