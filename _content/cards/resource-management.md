**What it is.** Card 27 of GM's deck, in "Resources and economy": spend limited things to get what you want. The deck's examples are *Civilization* and *Catan*. Its close relative in the inventory is *bottleneck inputs* (row 90, TH from Thorstad's "Against the Singularity Hypothesis"): every AI upgrade needs one of each input, and the scarcest one sets the pace.

**How it plays.** GM's two-minute version: each player gets ten paper clips, every action costs some, and the table sets the prices first. In the AI game: energy, water and money in *Data Center* and *Normal Technology*, money and energy in *The Committee*.

**Where it is in the week.** Most of the economy prototypes are this mechanic with something added. [Prices that move](prices-that-move.md) puts GPUs, power and land on a price curve with the AI first in line. [Every tap is a row](every-tap-is-a-row.md) races for the four parts of a data center with too little Water. [The deadline](the-deadline.md) trades tokens against an AI buyer. [Leaks](leaks.md) converts power and water into compute while the costs land elsewhere. SW's [Every resource names a place](every-resource-names-a-place.md) proposes taking the AI game's resources out of the bank and putting them on the map.

**What it does for learning.** The inventory's note on AI: the AI competes with people for the same energy and water, which is Bostrom's instrumental resources and Crawford's material AI at once. The bottleneck is the other side: physical limits slow a runaway, and whoever controls the scarce input controls the pace. Students can feel both in two minutes.

**Which of Marlon's ideas it serves.**
