Roger Caillois, *Man, Play and Games* (1958), answering Huizinga: play is free, separate, uncertain, unproductive, rule-bound and make-believe. It comes in four kinds, agôn (competition), alea (chance), mimicry (role play) and ilinx (vertigo), each on a line from paidia, free improvisation, to ludus, play bound by conventions. No copy of Caillois's book could be opened; everything here comes through Thomas Henricks's 2010 essay, and only the words in quotation marks are Caillois's.

---

> Caillois's definition has six elements. Play is (1) free—that is, nonobligatory; (2) separate—that is, cut off in the ways described above; (3) uncertain—in the sense that the results are not known beforehand; (4) unproductive—that is, an expenditure that does not create wealth or goods; (5) rule bound; and (6) fictive—that is, it is “accompanied by a special awareness of a second reality or of a free unreality, as against real life” (2001b, 9–10).

Thomas S. Henricks, "Caillois's *Man, Play, and Games*: An Appreciation and Evaluation," *American Journal of Play* 3, no. 2 (2010), p. 166. The list is Henricks's summary; the last phrase is Caillois's (his pp. 9–10).

> For example, he argues early on that games “are not ruled and make-believe. Rather they are ruled or make-believe” (2001b, 9). Similarly, he believes that competitive games (the agôn that Huizinga stressed) and games of chance are opposite affairs. In the former, the player “tries to vanquish a rival operating under the same conditions as himself”: in the latter, the player “merely awaits the outcome” (2001b, 12). His famous typology of games, then, follows: agôn (competition), alea (chance), mimicry (simulation or role play), and ilinx (balance or vertigo).
>
> Although Caillois develops these four categories, he does not claim that his list is exhaustive. Rather, the four types suggest one scheme that places play activities into “quadrants, each governed by an original principle.” [...] examples from all four types can be placed “on a continuum between two opposite poles.” One of these extremes is termed paidia, the principle of “diversion, turbulence, free improvisation, and carefree gaiety.” The other is ludus, the tendency to bind this capriciousness with “arbitrary, imperative, and purposively tedious conventions.” When this latter principle is applied to the freer forms of play, it calls out in the player a “greater amount of effort, patience, skill, or ingenuity” (2001b, 13).

Henricks, p. 167, quoting Caillois's pp. 9, 12 and 13. The cut drops part of one of Henricks's sentences.

> The final type of play is ilinx, the pursuit of vertigo, which consists “of an attempt to momentarily destroy the stability of perception and inflict a kind of voluptuous panic upon an otherwise lucid mind” (2001b, 23).

Henricks, p. 169, quoting Caillois's p. 23.

**What this gives the week, as KR reads it.** The four kinds are a checklist for any game the LLUFs build or remix: most classroom games are agôn, and chance draws and role play are often missing. Paidia to ludus is the line a session moves along as free play hardens into a rule set.
