Sometimes, a little. A 2023 meta-analysis finds small effects of games on attitudes, with half the studies at high risk of bias. Well-known successes sit beside nulls and reversals: playing the inspector in *Papers, Please* made players less willing to help immigrants. The few studies that separate the rules from the story suggest rules argue mostly when a story tells players how to read them.

---

**The answer to** [Does procedural rhetoric persuade?](does-procedural-rhetoric-persuade.md), from a research pass on Nov 16; the full note is `W11/research/persuasive-games-evidence.md`.

**The pooled evidence.** Kolek, Ropovik, Šisler, van Oostendorp and Brom (2023), "Video games and attitude change": explicit attitudes g = 0.24 across 101 effects, implicit g = 0.36 across 18, half the studies at high risk of bias, and the explicit effect near zero after adjusting for bias. The coders could not reliably tell "procedural rhetorics" apart from reward and economic mechanics, so they merged them as "meaningful feedback." The research found no meta-analysis of persuasive or social-issue games alone.

**Successes, nulls, reversals.** *Darfur is Dying* (Peng, Lee and Heeter 2010): playing beat watching or reading on some measures of role-taking and willingness to help; a larger 2017 study (634 people) did not find the play-versus-watch effect again. *Spent*, *PeaceMaker* and *My Cotton Picking Life* also show gains on some measures. *EnerCities* did no better than a text (Soekarjo and van Oostendorp 2015); UNHCR's *Against All Odds* showed no gain in three studies; *Bury Me, My Love* raised anxiety and lowered willingness to help. And *Papers, Please*, playing the border inspector, lowered players' intention and confidence to help immigrants (Peña et al. 2018): the argument a player takes away can run against the designer's.

**Isolating the rules.** Weakening the picking mechanic in *My Cotton Picking Life* halved the attitude change (Jacobs, DiGRA 2019). A preregistered 2025 study crossing rules and narrative found the rules alone did nothing without the story (149 people). A procedural and a narrative game on the same topic had effects that could not be told apart (262 people).

**The critique.** Sicart's "Against Procedurality" (*Game Studies*, 2011), as quoted by others because the journal's server refused connections: "Proceduralism often disregards the importance of play and players as activities that have creative, performative properties" (in Hawreliak, 2012).

**For the class, as KR reads it.** Both sides of [Nguyen and Bogost pull apart](nguyen-and-bogost-pull-apart.md) get evidence: rules can carry an argument, and players' play decides what they take from it. *Papers, Please* is the case to tell students before they ask what the AI game argues, and the [debrief](the-debrief-is-where-play-becomes-learning.md) is where to ask whether the game persuaded them of what its designers meant.
