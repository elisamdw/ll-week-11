Both well-known AI-futures games run about four hours with eight to fourteen players. Teams of two or three, states and labs, secretly commit a couple of freeform actions each turn; a facilitator rules on them and rolls dice when the outcome is uncertain; a debrief reveals what was hidden. Their designers say the facilitating is the hard part and the debrief is "crucial." Shorter versions of two to three hours exist.

---

**The answer to** [How are AI-futures role-play games run?](how-are-ai-futures-role-plays-run.md), from a research pass on Nov 16; the full note is `W11/research/ai-futures-role-plays.md`.

**Intelligence Rising** (Avin, Gruetzemacher and Fox, since 2018; Cambridge's CSER, now the charity Technology Strategy Roleplay). Four or more teams: two states and two or more tech firms, two or three players each, usually 8 to 12 in all. "Games typically involve 4-8 turns, with each turn representing one or two years" (AIES 2020). Each turn, every team commits two freeform "policy actions" and an R&D allocation in secret, and the facilitator resolves them all at once (2024 paper, 43 games); the two-action limit "introduced an attention economy." Dice decide R&D, cyberattacks, US elections, and, in almost every game, the ending. A session with debrief takes about four hours.

**The AI 2027 exercise** (AI Futures Project). "The exercise takes 4 hours and works for 8–14 people"; it starts in April 2027 with a stolen superhuman coder (ai-2027.com). Roles, from event listings rather than the project: the US government, the lab's leadership and its safety team, China, the press, and the AIs themselves. A facilitator called "Reality" plays the rest of the world, "adjudicates contested actions," and rolls dice when the outcome is uncertain; "Turns are ~30 minutes, each 1-3 months of simulated time." No official rules are published.

**The method underneath.** Both resemble Chris Engle's matrix games (from 1988): a player argues for an action and its result, others argue back, an umpire sets the odds, a die decides. Easy to learn; "the magic is in the facilitation" (Schmitt, 2026).

**What the designers report.** The facilitator's "on-the-spot judgement" shapes the story and carries their biases, which the debrief is there to check. Players with more background get more from it; Intelligence Rising has run in seven university courses and with six student groups. In-person play is more engaged than online. Endgame negotiations "under time pressure usually fail." Shorter formats exist: RAND's "Day After AGI" exercises take two hours, and one player said a 1.5-hour game taught "approximately as much" as a six-hour one.

**For Nov 23, as KR reads it.** The secret two-action turn, simultaneous reveal, and a dice roll for the ending fit [the firms as the players](the-firms-are-the-players.md) and the [committee](the-committee.md) prototype. Who takes the facilitator's chair, the LL or William, is a choice to make with Marlon's Nov 11 note in mind that the workshop shouldn't feel to William like teaching in his place (`W11/builds.md`). No published account was found of one of these games run inside a single class period of under three hours.
