On Nov 14, in the voice chat, Marlon named a tension between C. Thi Nguyen's games as the art of agency and Ian Bogost's procedural rhetoric. In voice Claude's summary (handoff back, section 3): one stresses the player's freedom; the other implies that the interactions send a message or, "in limit case," indoctrinate or train the player (the inner quote is his).

Voice Claude then proposed that students should feel both sides, first by playing the game and then by designing it. Marlon:

> that makes sense.

Nguyen (*Games: Agency as Art*, 2020) treats a game as a way of handing someone a kind of agency to try on: the designer shapes the goals and abilities, and the player inhabits them. Bogost (*Persuasive Games*, 2007) treats the same shaping as an argument. The two descriptions fit the same object, which is what makes the pairing teachable.

The order matters, as CI reads it. Playing first, the students are inside the agency the game grants and may not notice the argument. Designing second, they hold the levers, and see that every choice of what players can do is also a claim. The Nov 15 voice post on "two sides of the game thing" takes the agency side further ([Games heighten agency](games-heighten-agency.md)).
