Students who make a game about course material learn the material as well as the making, from Kafai's fourth-grade fraction games onward. University evidence is mostly case studies. Remixing an existing game is a sound first step, but changing only its settings doesn't reach the design underneath. Playtesting is the part that turns designers toward their players. No study was found on remixing with an AI coding agent.

---

**The answer to** [What do students learn by designing games?](what-do-students-learn-by-designing-games.md), from a research pass on Nov 16; the full note is `W11/research/learning-by-designing-games.md`.

**The research line.** Kafai and Burke's 2015 review of 55 studies, "Constructionist Gaming," reports that making games teaches programming and, when the game is about course content, the content: Kafai's fourth graders who designed fraction games became "significantly better at understanding and representing fractions" than comparison students (the review's summary of her 1995 study). A 2018 review of 45 articles (Hava and Cakir) names programming, problem solving and creative thinking as the gains, and students' and teachers' inexperience with game-making as the main barrier. The research found no meta-analysis with a pooled effect size for designing games, as opposed to playing them.

**Players.** Dishon and Kafai (2020) found three features of design that support taking other people's perspectives: games are made for others, playtesting gives quick feedback, and ideas about players have to become concrete design choices.

**At university, in courses not about games.** Sandoz (2016): in a game-design assignment on social class, the prototypes showed students had grasped the structural side of inequality better than their written reports did, so the artifact was the evidence. Littrell et al. (2020), a 16-student honors course designing climate-collapse board games: 70% said they were highly engaged, only 56% said it helped them think critically about the topic, and a student wished they "had started to create them earlier in the semester." Closest to William's course: a 2026 American Sociological Association roundtable (Smith and Herron, Iowa) where students design board games that encode moral norms, power, rewards and sanctions; an abstract, with no results.

**Remixing.** Across 2.4 million Scratch projects, students who remixed more knew more programming concepts (Dasgupta et al. 2016). Remixes of popular, much-remixed projects tend to be less original (Hill and Monroy-Hernández 2013). Peppler and Kafai (2007): changing a game only within its preset parameters "does not touch the backbones of game design."

**For the two sessions, as KR reads it.** For Wednesday's [Remix a mechanic with Claude Code](remix-a-mechanic-with-claude-code.md): ask for a change that reaches a mechanic, not a number, and have another table play the remix before the end. For Nov 23's [game on the class's own database](design-a-game-on-the-classs-own-database.md): judge what students understood from the game itself as well as from their talk about it, and leave time for one round of playtesting.
