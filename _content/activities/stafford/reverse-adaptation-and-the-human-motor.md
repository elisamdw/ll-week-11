**Marlon, Nov 13 (dictated):** the week Stafford comes in is "where we'll reflect thematically on ideas of AI [running?] on its own, potentially beyond human control. That'll be the theme of the game."

**Established (cited, secondhand):** William assigns Winner's "Artifice and Order" and Rabinbach's "From Mimetic Machines to Digital Organisms" for the workshop day. Winner's claim (via the SEP and reading notes) is not that machines have wills but that large technical systems set their own requirements and people adjust their ends to fit the means: "reverse adaptation." Rabinbach's earlier *The Human Motor* treated the working body as an engine with fatigue as its limit; this chapter carries the figure forward to automata and digital organisms (from the publisher's description). Neither text has been opened by us.

**For the game (SW's proposal):** Two mechanics that make the readings playable rather than illustrated. (1) **Reverse adaptation** (TH's idea): when the AI builds in a faction's region, that faction rewrites one of its goals to suit the new infrastructure; nobody forces it. At the end, put each faction's first and last goals side by side. (2) **Fatigue:** human factions have an energy track that actions drain; AI nodes never tire. Labor can also `strike`, stopping a node for a round.

**Why it fits:** Fizek's idle games (in our reading set) already put the player in Winner's position: you set the system going and then watch it, adjusting yourself to what it needs. *Paperclips* does this to the player before class.

**Try it:** After one round of *Data Center*, ask each faction to write its goal again, and compare with the card it started with.

**Could go wrong:** Two long readings on workshop day; students may not have finished them. The mechanics should work for someone who hasn't read either.
