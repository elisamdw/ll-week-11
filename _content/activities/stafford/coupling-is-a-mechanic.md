**Established (cited):** On Nov 16 William assigns Perrow's "Complexity, Coupling, and Catastrophe" and Fisch's "Ninety Seconds." Perrow: in systems that are interactively complex and tightly coupled, accidents are normal, built in; better safety devices can add new paths to failure. Fisch: Tokyo's commuter network runs beyond capacity because people and control systems absorb the strain together, and his "margin of indeterminacy" is the slack that lets it. The chapter is about the 2005 Amagasaki derailment (TH's summary from a review; not opened).

**Why this is the easiest bridge (SW's reading):** Both readings describe a system in terms a game already has: links, timing, slack, a cascade. Pandemic's outbreaks, the Beer Game's bullwhip, and Mini Metro's overload all play it (RS's research). The LL's *Data Center* game already has a `cascade-failure` rule and a `harden` action.

**For the game (SW's proposal, after TH's):** Mark each link on the map tight or loose. A failure crosses a tight link in the same round, before any human acts, and a loose link the round after. Loosening a link costs something (money, speed, the AI's growth), so the choice of which links to loosen is the argument. Fisch adds a second dial: how much slack the people at a node can absorb before they fail too.

**Try it:** On paper, ten index cards and tape: tight links in red, loose in blue. Knock one card over and count how many fall this round.

**Could go wrong:** Students have just done these readings with William; the workshop should use them, not re-teach them. Let William name the concepts.
