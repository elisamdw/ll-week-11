**The history, already written:** HI's [Strand 1, the proving ground](../../history/1-proving-ground.md): chess, checkers, Go and video games as the benchmarks AI was measured by. The two items most worth ten minutes of class: [Deep Blue and Kasparov](../../history/items/deep-blue-kasparov.md), and [AlphaGo](../../history/items/alphago.md), where DeepMind says Move 37 in game 2 "had a 1 in 10,000 chance of being used," and Lee Sedol's Move 78 in game 4, the one human win, had the same odds (HI's item, with the two match videos).

**The turn that students like:** Wang et al. (2022, ICML 2023) trained an adversary that beats superhuman Go programs with a strategy an amateur human can see through ([TH's entry](../../bibliography/wang-et-al-2022-adversarial-policies-beat-superhuman-go-ais.md)). A machine can be superhuman and still have a blind spot; the mechanic [`move-78`](../../mechanics/README.md) in our inventory turns that into a rule.

**Three ways a machine sits at a table (TD's framing):** as an **opponent** (Deep Blue, AlphaGo), as an **advisor** whispering a move (the `ai-advisor` mechanic), and as a **faction whose rules are written down** (the AI in our *Data Center* is a deck of cards, not a model: it plays by procedure). Students can try all three on Wednesday, the third without any model at all.

**Why it matters for this class:** the term has used AI as a tool and a collaborator. Here it is a player, which raises a question they can test in an afternoon: what does it change about a game when one seat is a machine, and do you play it differently once you know?
