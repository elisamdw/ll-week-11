Marlon, Nov 14 (voice chat): he sees a tension between Nguyen's games as the art of agency and Bogost's procedural rhetoric, where the interactions send a message or, "in limit case," indoctrinate or train the player. On Bogost: "it's through the play that the argument is made." He agreed students should feel both sides, first playing, then designing ("that makes sense"; the framing was the voice Claude's).

**The two sides, already written by TH:** [Nguyen](../../theory/nguyen-games-agency-as-art.md) (games as a medium for agency; the designer sculpts what you care about while you play); [Bogost](../../theory/bogost-persuasive-games.md) (procedural rhetoric: an argument made by the rules of behaviour a game sets); [Sicart, *Play Matters*](../../theory/sicart-play-matters.md) (the player's reply to procedurality); [Flanagan](../../theory/flanagan-critical-play.md) (play as critique: unplaying, reskinning, rewriting).

**A pair that makes the tension playable:** our game ships in two versions on one engine. *Data Center* argues the Bostrom story (an AI spreads, a hidden card is the turn); *Normal Technology* argues Narayanan and Kapoor's (it goes only where people adopt it, and there is no turn). Same board, same factions, different claim. See the [game](../../game/README.md).

**For the class (TD's proposal):** one question to carry through the day: *when you remixed it, did you give the player more choices, or make a different argument?* Students will usually do both; the point is to notice which.
