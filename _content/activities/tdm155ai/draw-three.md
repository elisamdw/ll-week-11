**What students do**

1. **Draw three (1 min).** Each table draws three cards, face up.
2. **Design (12 min).** Make a paper game that uses all three. Write it on one index card: the goal, what a turn is, and the three mechanics named. Claude may help as a rules-writer or a sceptic ("find the loophole in this"), but the game must be playable with what's on the table.
3. **Play once (6 min).**
4. **Swap one card (6 min).** Draw a fourth card, swap it for one of the three, change the game, and play one more round. Apply Sid Meier's test ("interesting decisions") or the inventory's `token-test`: did the new card add a decision, or only a token to move?

**What it teaches:** design by constraint, at the speed of play. Mechanics combine and collide; the swap shows how one part changes the whole (Marlon, Nov 2: "Games are complex; they involve many, many parts"). The index card is the first form of a game's template, which the next activities put into data and code.

**Prepare:** the deck shuffled per table; a box of paper bits (index cards, dice, coins, tokens). GM's README proposes this as one of three ways to play the deck.

**Try it:** draw *Push your luck*, *Trading and negotiation* and *Hidden role*; most tables end up with a small bluffing market.

**What could go wrong:** a table stalls on a combination that won't fit. Let them redraw one card after three minutes. Keep Claude out of the first five minutes so the table designs before it asks.
